Energy — the lens that unifies all of it

Lesson 2 left one word undefined: graded. This lesson defines it properly, and the definition turns out to be an old friend from your statistics degree wearing a physicist's coat.

1 · One number: how well do these two things go together?

Forget architectures for a moment. LeCun asks you to picture a machine that does exactly one thing. You hand it a pair — a past x and a candidate future y — and it returns a single number.

The number means incompatibility. A low number says "yes, that future goes with that past." A high number says "no, those do not belong together." That number is called the energy, and the machine that computes it is an energy function, written E(x, y).

That is the whole primitive. Not a probability, not a prediction — a compatibility score. Watch what it buys us.

🗣️ Plain: one number saying how badly these two things fit together. Small means they fit. STATa scalar-valued function of a data pair; a fitted measure of (in)compatibility, unnormalised
CS/MLan energy function E(x, y) — the model outputs a scalar energy for each input pair

2 · The stats translation — and the term that vanishes

You have met this object before under a different name. If you write a conditional density in the standard exponential form, energy is just the exponent:

p(y | x) = exp( −E(x, y) ) / Z(x)

Rearranged: E(x, y) = −log p(y | x) − log Z(x). So energy is negative log-likelihood, up to that Z(x) term. Maximum likelihood and minimum energy are the same instinct.

Now look hard at Z(x). It is the normalising constant — the sum or integral of exp(−E(x, y)) over every possible y. For text that is a sum over a vocabulary of maybe 50,000 tokens; a softmax does it, no problem. For the next second of video, y lives in a space of millions of continuous dimensions, and that integral cannot be computed. Ever.

Why LeCun works in energy and not probability A probability must be normalised, so it must integrate over every possible future. An energy has no such duty. It only has to be lower here than there. Drop the demand to normalise, and high-dimensional continuous prediction becomes tractable.
🗣️ Plain: the bookkeeping factor that forces probabilities to add up to one — and the thing that is impossible to compute in a huge continuous space. STATthe normalising constant / partition function Z(x); intractable for high-dimensional continuous y
CS/MLthe partition function — energy-based models are deliberately unnormalised models, which is what makes them usable here

3 · The blur, finally explained at the root

Here is the payoff for Lesson 1 and Lesson 2. Ask the pen question again: the pen is toppling, and it may land left or right.

A pixel-predicting model must emit one y. Graded on squared error, the single best y is the average of both — the ghost pen, the blur. It has no vocabulary for "either of these two."

An energy function has no such problem. It can assign low energy to pen-left and low energy to pen-right at the same time, and high energy to the blurred pen in between. Multiple good answers are natural to it, because it never had to pick one, and never had to split a fixed probability mass between them.

The one-sentence version to memorise A predictor must name one future, so it averages them. An energy function only has to rank futures, so it can call several of them good at once — which is exactly what uncertainty needs.

4 · So what is training?

If the model is just a landscape of energies, then training is landscape sculpting, and it has exactly two moves:

  • Push the energy down on pairs you actually observed in the data. Those futures really did follow those pasts, so they must score as compatible.
  • Push the energy up everywhere else — or the landscape goes flat.

That second move is the entire difficulty, and it is the same problem as Lesson 2's cheat. If you only ever push down, the model discovers it can set the energy to zero everywhere. Every future is then perfectly compatible with every past, the loss is minimal, and nothing has been learned. In Lesson 2 you met this as representation collapse. In energy language it is a flat energy landscape — the same failure, seen from a different angle.

There are two families of cures, and the names are worth having now:

  • Contrastive methods — manufacture wrong pairs on purpose and push their energy up explicitly. Simple, but you need a great many wrong pairs, and the number you need grows badly as dimensions grow.
  • Regularised methods — never name a wrong pair. Instead limit the volume of the space that is allowed to have low energy, so pushing one region down automatically lifts the rest. LeCun prefers this family, and it is the route JEPA takes.
🗣️ Plain: two ways to stop the model calling everything compatible — either point at bad examples, or cap how much of the space is allowed to look good. STATcontrastive estimation vs. a capacity/volume penalty on the fitted score surface
CS/MLcontrastive methods (negative samples) vs. regularised methods (information/volume constraints, e.g. VICReg-style terms)

5 · Where this sits in LeCun's argument

Energy is the frame that makes the whole course one argument rather than three. The world model of Lesson 1, the JEPA boxes of Lesson 2, the collapse problem, and the planning machinery still ahead are all descriptions of one energy landscape and how it is sculpted. When a JEPA is trained, the prediction error in representation space is the energy. When an agent later plans, it searches for the action sequence with the lowest energy. Same number, two jobs.

Q. Why does LeCun build on energy functions instead of probability distributions for predicting the physical world?
