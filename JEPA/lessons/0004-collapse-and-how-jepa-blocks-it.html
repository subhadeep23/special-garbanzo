Collapse — and how JEPA blocks it

Lesson 3 said the hard half of training is "push the energy up everywhere else." This lesson is about doing that without ever naming a single bad example — the trick that makes the whole approach work.

0 · Spaced recall — answer before reading on

Three questions from the last two lessons. Do them from memory; the effort is the point.

  • Draw JEPA's three boxes. What goes into each, and what is compared to what?
  • What does a low energy E(x, y) mean, in plain words?
  • Why can't we just work with p(y | x) instead?

If the third one came slowly: the normalising constant Z(x) is an integral over every possible future, and it cannot be computed in a high-dimensional continuous space.

1 · Two ways a model can learn nothing

You already met the obvious failure. If training only ever rewards s_x's prediction for matching s_y, the encoders can conspire: output the same constant vector for every input. The prediction is then perfect, the loss is zero, and the model has learned exactly nothing. That is complete collapse, and in energy language it is a landscape that is flat — every future is equally compatible with every past.

There is a second, sneakier version, and it is the one that bites in practice. The embeddings are not all identical, but they all lie squashed inside a thin slice of the available space — say the vectors have 1024 dimensions but only genuinely vary along 20 of them. The other 1004 dimensions are dead weight, or are near-duplicates of each other. Nothing looks broken. The loss goes down. But most of the representation's capacity is gone.

🗣️ Plain: the summaries are not identical, but they all crowd into a thin sliver of the space, so most of the room goes unused. STATthe embedding covariance matrix is near-singular — most variance sits in a few directions; the effective rank is far below the nominal dimension
CS/MLdimensional collapse (VICReg calls the correlated version informational collapse)

Both failures come from the same source: a loss that only rewards agreement. Agreement is cheapest when there is nothing to agree about.

2 · The obvious fix, and why LeCun avoids it

The direct answer is to name bad pairs on purpose. Take this image's summary and that unrelated image's summary, and push their energy up. Now the model cannot flatten the landscape, because you are actively holding parts of it high. Those manufactured bad pairs are called negative samples, and this is the contrastive family from Lesson 3.

It works. It is also, in LeCun's view, the wrong bet for the physical world, for two reasons worth holding on to:

  • The count scales badly. To pin down a surface you need points that surround it. In one dimension a handful will do. In a space of hundreds of dimensions, the number of negatives needed to hem the low-energy region in from every side grows out of reach. You end up sampling a vanishing fraction of the space you are trying to hold up.
  • Your negatives are often lies. Two "unrelated" video clips of a street may genuinely be near-identical in meaning. Pushing them apart teaches the model something false. This is the false negative problem, and it gets worse the better your data is.
The move that defines modern JEPA Stop trying to push energy up at specific points. Instead, constrain the model so that only a limited volume of the space is allowed to have low energy at all. Then pushing one region down automatically lifts everywhere else — for free, with no negatives. This is the regularised family.

3 · Cure A — break the symmetry

Look again at why collapse is possible. Both encoders are being pulled toward each other by the same gradient, so they can walk to the same constant together, hand in hand. Deny them that and the shortcut closes. Two devices do it:

  • Stop-gradient. The target branch produces s_y, but no gradient is allowed to flow back through it. The target is treated as a fixed fact for that step. The predictor must move toward the target; the target cannot slide down to meet the predictor.
  • An EMA target encoder. The target encoder's weights are not trained directly at all. They are a slow exponential moving average of the context encoder's weights — a lagging copy. So the target the model chases is always a slightly stale version of itself, which keeps the two branches from racing to the same trivial answer.
🗣️ Plain: freeze one side so it can't move toward the other, and make that side a slow-motion copy of the side that is learning. STATan exponentially weighted moving average of the parameter sequence — a heavily smoothed, lagged estimate
CS/MLstop-gradient plus an EMA (momentum) target encoder — the asymmetry introduced by BYOL, and the mechanism I-JEPA uses

This is empirically effective and slightly mysterious: it prevents collapse without any explicit term in the loss saying "don't collapse". It is a structural cure, not a stated one.

4 · Cure B — say it out loud in the loss

The other route adds terms that make collapse expensive. VICReg is the clearest example, and its name is literally its three terms:

Term What it asks for Which failure it kills
Variance each dimension's spread across the batch must stay above a threshold complete collapse — a constant has zero spread, so it is forbidden outright
Invariance the prediction must match the target none — this is the actual learning signal
Covariance different dimensions must be uncorrelated with each other dimensional collapse — duplicated dimensions carry no extra information

Read that as a statistician and it is very familiar furniture. The variance term is a floor on each coordinate's standard deviation. The covariance term drives the off-diagonal entries of the embedding covariance matrix toward zero — it is a decorrelation penalty, close in spirit to asking for a whitened representation.

🗣️ Plain: force every dimension to actually vary, and force them to vary about different things. STATa lower bound on each marginal standard deviation, plus a penalty on the off-diagonal covariances — a decorrelation / near-whitening constraint
CS/MLVICReg — Variance-Invariance-Covariance Regularization. Barlow Twins is a close relative: push the cross-correlation matrix toward the identity

5 · Back to the energy picture

Now cash in Lesson 3's framing, because this is where the whole course knits together. Every cure above is one answer to the same question: how do you raise the energy everywhere else without naming a single bad pair?

  • The variance floor forbids the flat landscape directly — the constant solution is simply made illegal.
  • The covariance penalty forbids the thin sliver — the low-energy region is not allowed to be a degenerate subspace.
  • The stop-gradient and EMA asymmetry denies the shortcut path that leads to the flat solution in the first place.
The one-sentence version to memorise Contrastive methods hold the energy up at points you choose. Regularised methods cap the volume of space allowed to be low-energy, so the energy rises everywhere else on its own.

That is why the two families are the right way to carve up the field, and why the word "collapse" keeps appearing in every JEPA paper. It is not a bug that occasionally happens. It is the trivial solution the objective genuinely prefers, and every design choice in a JEPA is, in some measure, a wall built against it.
