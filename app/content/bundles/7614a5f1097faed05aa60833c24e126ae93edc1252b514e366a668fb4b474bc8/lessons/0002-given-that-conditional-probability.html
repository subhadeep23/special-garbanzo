1 · Two cups

The stall owner from L1 is back. He tosses two coins, hides each under a cup, and peeks under both.

"I'll tell you one thing," he says. "At least one of them is heads. Now: bet ₹10 that both are heads, and I'll pay you ₹25 if you're right."

Your puzzle · 3 minutes, pen and paper
  1. Before his news, list the four ways two coins can land.
  2. Cross out every way his news rules out.
  3. How many ways are left? In how many are both coins heads?

Voice A: "One coin is heads. The other coin doesn't know that. It's 50–50, so both heads is 1/2."

Voice B: "Only TT is ruled out. Three ways are left, and just one is HH. So it's 1/3."

Pick a voice. Then decide: is the bet worth taking?

2 · The plain idea: news shrinks the bag

Roll a fair die. The chance of a 3 is 1 in 6.

Now someone tells you: "the result was an odd number." Nothing about the die changed — but your knowledge did. Odd faces are {1, 3, 5}. The other three faces are now impossible, so they leave the picture entirely.

You are no longer choosing from six equally likely faces. You are choosing from three. So the chance of a 3 is now 1 in 3.

The whole idea in one sentence

New information does not change the world — it shrinks the sample space down to the outcomes still consistent with what you were told, and you re-do the count inside that smaller bag.

Back to the cups: Voice B wins. The news didn't touch the coins. It threw TT out of the bag. Three equally likely ways are left (HH, HT, TH), and one of them is HH. So the chance is 1/3. Three bets: you lose ₹10 twice and win ₹25 once. That's ₹5 ahead, so take it. Voice A's mistake: "the other coin" isn't a fixed coin. The news doesn't say which cup holds the head.

That is conditional probability. "Conditional" simply means on the condition that we already know something. We write it with a vertical bar:

P(B | A)  =  "the probability of B, given that A has happened"

Read the bar out loud as the word "given". P(3 | odd) is just "chance of a 3, given it was odd". The bar is not division and it is not "or" — it is the word "given".

Zooming in on a map. Draw the sample space as a map, with each event a region whose area is its probability. Being told A is like zooming in until region A fills the whole screen.

The mapProbability
the whole mapthe sample space
a region's areaan event's chance
zoom until A fills the screenlearning "A happened"
what left the screenoutcomes the news ruled out
how much of the screen B coversP(B | A)

Every region inside A keeps its shape and its share of A. That's why the answer is B's overlap with A, as a share of A.

Where it breaks: you can't zoom into a region of zero area. If P(A) = 0, there is no screen to fill, and P(B | A) is simply not defined by this formula.

3 · Where the formula comes from

Let's redo the die example slowly, and the formula will appear by itself.

Being told A ("odd") means we only care about the part of the world where A is true. Inside that part, we want the outcomes where B ("a 3") is also true. So we need two counts:

What we needIn the die exampleAs a probability
outcomes where A and B both holdjust {3}P(A ∩ B) = 1/6
outcomes where A holds at all{1, 3, 5}P(A) = 3/6

The answer is the first as a share of the second: (1/6) ÷ (3/6) = 1/3. Which is exactly what we said in plain words. So:

P(B | A) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(A)

defined whenever P(A) > 0 — you cannot condition on something impossible

Two things to notice, because both are marks in an exam:

1The symbol ∩ means "and" — both events happen together. Many books write it as just P(AB), with no symbol at all. Same thing.
2Dividing by P(A) is the re-normalising step. The old bag totalled 1; the new smaller bag only totalled P(A). Dividing scales it back up so the new bag totals 1 again.

4 · A worked example — do this one with me

Two fair dice are thrown. Given that the total is divisible by 5, what is the chance that both dice show a five?

Take it in three steps, and never skip the first one.

Step 1 — name the events. Let A = "the total is divisible by 5". Let B = "both dice show five".

Step 2 — find P(A). Out of 36 equally likely pairs, which totals are divisible by 5? Totals of 5 and of 10. Total 5 happens 4 ways (1-4, 2-3, 3-2, 4-1); total 10 happens 3 ways (4-6, 5-5, 6-4). That is 7 pairs, so P(A) = 7/36.

Step 3 — find P(A ∩ B). "Both show five" is the single pair 5-5, and its total is 10, which is divisible by 5. So A and B hold together in exactly 1 of 36 cases: P(A ∩ B) = 1/36.

Answer. P(B | A) = (1/36) ÷ (7/36) = 1/7.

Now watch the same calculation as a picture. Every pair is a cell; the news throws most of them out.

Notice the shape of the work. The 36 cancelled. Conditional probability questions are, almost always, counting inside the smaller bag — here, 1 good case out of the 7 cases that survived the news.

In one or two sentences of your own: before any news, the chance of 5-5 was 1/36. Why did it jump to 1/7, when the dice themselves didn't change at all?

Compare with a model answer

The news threw out 29 of the 36 pairs, and 5-5 was not one of them. The same single good pair now competes with only 7 survivors instead of 36, so its share of the bag grew from 1/36 to 1/7. The dice are the same; what we know about them changed.

Your turn, half done. Two fair dice are rolled. Given that at least one shows a 6, what is the chance that both show a 6?

Step 1: A = "at least one 6". Out of 36 pairs, 6 have a 6 first and 6 have a 6 second, and 6-6 is in both lists, so A has ____ pairs. Step 2: "both 6" and A together is just the pair ____. Step 3: so the answer is 1 / ____ .

Check your steps

6 + 6 − 1 = 11 pairs survive the news. Only 6-6 has both dice showing 6, and it is inside A. So P(both 6 | at least one 6) = 1/11, not 1/6: most people forget that "at least one" lets the six sit on either die.

5 · Read the formula backwards: the multiplication rule

Move P(A) to the other side and you get a second tool, which is used even more often than the first:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A)

In words: the chance both happen = the chance the first happens, times the chance the second happens once the first already has. This is how you handle anything drawn without replacement, where each draw changes what is left.

Example. An urn holds 6 red and 4 black balls. Two are drawn without replacement. What is the chance both are red?

First ball red: 6/10. Now 9 balls remain and only 5 are red, so second ball red given the first was red: 5/9.

P(both red) = (6/10) × (5/9) = 30/90 = 1/3

The rule extends as far as you need, one factor per draw, each conditioned on everything before it:

P(A1 ∩ A2 ∩ A3) = P(A1) × P(A2 | A1) × P(A3 | A1 ∩ A2)

This is called the chain rule (or multiplication theorem). It holds as long as every event you condition on has non-zero probability.

6 · Independence — when the news changes nothing

Sometimes you learn A, and your view of B does not move at all. Toss a coin twice: knowing the first toss was heads tells you nothing about the second. In symbols, P(B | A) = P(B).

Put that into the multiplication rule and it simplifies beautifully:

A and B are independent  ⇔  P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)

That product form is the definition examiners use, because it stays valid even when P(A) = 0 and the "given" version would break. Events that satisfy it are called independent events.

"Mutually exclusive means independent: the two events have nothing to do with each other." Wrong. Independent and mutually exclusive are not the same thing. They are close to opposites.

  • Mutually exclusive means they cannot both happen: P(A ∩ B) = 0. So learning A happened tells you B definitely did not — that is a huge amount of information.
  • Independent means learning A tells you nothing about B.

So two events with real probabilities that are mutually exclusive are, for that very reason, not independent. Check: if they were independent, P(A)P(B) would have to equal 0, which forces one of them to be impossible.

A concrete case: one die roll, A = "a 1", B = "a 2". They can't both happen, so P(A ∩ B) = 0. But P(A) × P(B) = 1/36, which is not 0. Not independent.

See the difference as area. The square below has area 1, and each event is a region inside it.

7 · Optional deeper layer — skip this on a busy week

Only if you're curious today. A standard theory question asks you to show that conditional probability is itself a probability. The point is that P(· | A) obeys the same three rules from Lesson 1:

  • Never negative — it's a non-negative number divided by a positive one.
  • Totals one — the whole sample space, given A, is just A itself, and P(A) ÷ P(A) = 1.
  • Separate things add — if events don't overlap, neither do their overlaps with A, so the additions carry through the division unchanged.

The consequence is the useful part: every rule you already know stays true after conditioning. The complement rule still works, so P(not B | A) = 1 − P(B | A). You are not learning a new system — you are working in the old one, in a smaller bag.

Your pocket question · carry it for a few days

Three cups. A ₹100 note is under one of them. You point to cup 1. The owner knows where the note is. He lifts one of the other two cups and shows it's empty. He always does this, and he always lets you switch. "Keep cup 1, or switch to the last cup?"

Most people say it doesn't matter: two cups left, 50–50. Is that Voice A again? Think about it on the bus. Take one hint at a time.

Hint 1 (after a day)

List the three places the note could be, each with chance 1/3. For each, write which cup the owner lifts, and what happens if you switch.

Hint 2 (after another day)

The owner never lifts the cup with the note. So his choice isn't random: it's news that depends on where the note is. Does keeping cup 1 ever use that news?

The answer (try for three days first)

Switch. Keeping wins only if your first pick was right, which has chance 1/3; the owner's show changes nothing about that. Switching wins in the other two cases, so 2/3. The news shrank the bag, but not evenly, because the owner chose what to show knowing the answer. This is the famous Monty Hall problem. Even some professional mathematicians got it wrong when it appeared in a magazine in 1990.

Conditioning is how all of statistics learns from data. You shrink the bag to what you were told, then share the chances out again.

WhereThe chance it wants

Each row is the same bag-shrinking move you just learned.

The names, and the ISI version

A spam filter is a classifier. A chain of "next, given now" chances is a Markov chain. The chance of the data given a model is the likelihood.

Our formula divides by P(B), so it fails when P(B) = 0. At ISI depth, conditional expectation given a σ-field handles even that case. The bag-shrinking picture is still the one to keep.

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

Show that if A and B are independent, then so are A and Bᶜ ("not B"). In words: if learning B tells you nothing about A, then learning "not B" tells you nothing either.

Worked proof

A splits into two non-overlapping pieces: the part inside B and the part outside it. By Lesson 1's Rule 3,

P(A) = P(A ∩ B) + P(A ∩ Bᶜ)

Independence gives P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B), so

P(A ∩ Bᶜ) = P(A) − P(A)P(B) = P(A)(1 − P(B))

and 1 − P(B) = P(Bᶜ) by the complement rule. That is the product rule for A and Bᶜ. ∎

Apply it twice and Aᶜ and Bᶜ are independent too. That is why "the chance neither happens" for independent events is just (1 − P(A))(1 − P(B)).

An ISI entrance problem

Here is a real ISI question, in plain words first. Twin brothers Bikas and Bimal play tennis. Bikas wins each set with chance 0.6, and sets don't affect each other. The first to win three sets wins the match. Suppose the match goes all five sets. What is the chance that Bikas wins?

Source: ISI JRF Statistics 2024 paper (STA), Q4(a). Official wording, after that set-up: "If the match lasts for five sets, what is the probability that Bikas will win the match?"

Worked solution

Name the events. F = "the match lasts five sets", W = "Bikas wins the match".

Shrink the bag. The match reaches a fifth set exactly when the first four sets are split 2–2. So F is an event about sets 1–4 only. Given F, whoever wins set 5 wins the match.

Use independence. Set 5 is independent of sets 1–4. So learning F tells you nothing about it:

P(W | F) = P(Bikas wins set 5) = 0.6

Check with the formula. Write K for a set Bikas wins and M for one Bimal wins. These are all the 2–2 splits of four sets:

Sets 1–4Chance of that order
KKMM0.6 × 0.6 × 0.4 × 0.4
KMKM0.6 × 0.4 × 0.6 × 0.4
KMMK0.6 × 0.4 × 0.4 × 0.6
MKKM0.4 × 0.6 × 0.6 × 0.4
MKMK0.4 × 0.6 × 0.4 × 0.6
MMKK0.4 × 0.4 × 0.6 × 0.6

Every order has the same chance, 0.6² × 0.4², because independent sets multiply. There are 6 orders, so

P(F) = 6 × 0.6² × 0.4² = 216/625

and P(W ∩ F) = P(F) × 0.6. Dividing gives 3/5 = 0.6.

Checked by exact enumeration with fractions: P(Bikas wins | five sets) = 3/5. Part (b) of the same question turns the bar around, and it is the ISI problem in Lesson 3.
