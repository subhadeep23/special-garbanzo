Uniform and Exponential — Flat Chances and Forgetful Waits

Your first two named densities, and how the Poisson hides inside one. ~22 min.

TODAY'S WIN  You'll be able to use U(a, b) for "equally likely anywhere in a stretch", use the exponential for "waiting time with no memory", switch cleanly between its rate and its mean, and turn a Poisson count question into an exponential waiting-time question and back.

0 · Spaced recall — no peeking

• Rolling until the first six: given 4 misses so far, what is P(at least 3 more rolls are needed)?

• Calls arrive at 6 per hour. What is λ for a 10-minute window?

Answers: needing at least 3 more rolls means the next 2 miss: (5/6)² = 25/36; the misses already seen don't matter (L10). And λ = 6 × 1/6 = 1 (L9).

1 · Uniform: flat chances

A bus will arrive at some moment in the next 10 minutes, and no moment is more likely than another. The arrival time X is spread evenly over the stretch, like sand levelled flat (L5). Its density is a rectangle: the uniform distribution U(a, b) has

f(x) = 1/(b − a) for a ≤ x ≤ b,  and 0 elsewhere.

The height is forced: the area, height × width, must be 1. Probabilities are just lengths in proportion: P(c < X < d) = (d − c)/(b − a) for a stretch inside [a, b]. The balance point is the middle, (a + b)/2 (L6), and the variance is

Var(X) = (b − a)²/12

from E[X²] = (a² + ab + b²)/3 minus the squared mean (L7). For the bus, U(0, 10): mean 5 minutes, variance 100/12 ≈ 8.33, SD about 2.9 minutes.

2 · Exponential: the forgetful wait

Now a help line where calls arrive Poisson-style, at an average rate of 3 per hour (L9). How long until the first call? Call that wait T. Here is the key step, and it needs no new tool: the first call comes after time t exactly when no call lands in the window from 0 to t. That window expects λt = 3t calls, so

P(T > t) = P(Poisson(3t) = 0) = e−3t

Slide the window and predict first.

A wait with P(T > t) = e−λt for t ≥ 0 has the exponential distribution with rate λ. Its distribution function is F(t) = 1 − e−λt and its density is the slope (L5):

f(t) = λe−λt,  t ≥ 0

Its mean is 1/λ: 3 calls an hour means a 20-minute wait on average, which is just common sense run backwards. Its variance is 1/λ², so the SD equals the mean. (Go deeper gets both from a continuous tail-sum.)

RATE OR MEAN?  Books write the same distribution two ways. Rate λ: f(t) = λe−λt, mean 1/λ. Mean μ: f(t) = (1/μ)e−t/μ, rate 1/μ. Your corpus uses the mean form for the exponential, and then, two pages on, uses μ for the Poisson rate when linking the two, so the same letter means "mean" in one place and "rate" in the other. Always ask: is this number "per hour" (a rate) or "hours" (a mean)?

3 · The wait forgets

A bulb's life is exponential with mean 1000 hours. Does a bulb that has already burned for 1000 hours have a worse chance of lasting another 500 than a new one? Compare with a uniform life of the same mean.

P(T > s + t | T > s) = e−λ(s+t)/e−λs = e−λt = P(T > t)

Say it in one line

The exponential is the continuous geometric: the waiting time between Poisson events, with survival P(T > t) = e−λt, mean 1/λ, and the same memoryless property as L10. It is the only continuous distribution with no memory.

A radioactive atom. An atom of radon doesn't age. Whether it was created a second ago or a week ago, its chance of decaying in the next minute is the same. That's why its lifetime is exactly exponential and why "half-life" makes sense: after ln 2/λ, half of any sample has decayed, and half of what's left decays in the next half-life, for ever.

Where it breaks: bulbs, machines and people do age: an old car is more likely to break down this month than a new one. For them the exponential is only a first approximation, and reliability theory replaces it with models whose failure rate changes with age (the Weibull, the gamma of L14).

"Exponential with mean 1000 hours has density 1000e−1000t." That mixes up the rate and the mean, and it's the most common exponential mistake. Mean 1000 hours means rate 1/1000 per hour, so f(t) = (1/1000)e−t/1000. The wrong version has mean 1/1000 hour, about 3.6 seconds. A one-second check catches it: the mean must come out in the units of time, and a big mean must mean a slowly decaying density.

4 · Worked examples

(a) A bulb's life is exponential with mean 1000 h. Find P(T > 1500), and, given it has lasted 1000 h, the chance it lasts 500 more. (b) Calls arrive at 3 per hour. Find the chance of no call in a 20-minute break, two ways. (c) X ~ U(0, 10). Compare P(X > 7 | X > 4) with P(X > 3).

(a) Rate 1/1000, so

P(T > 1500) = e−1500/1000 = e−1.5 = 0.2231

Given 1000 hours survived, memorylessness says the remaining life is a fresh exponential, the same as a new bulb's:

P(T > 1500 | T > 1000) = P(T > 500) = e−0.5 = 0.6065

(b) Count way: 20 minutes is 1/3 hour, so the count is Poisson(1) and P(no call) = e−1 = 0.3679. Wait way: T ~ exponential with rate 3 per hour, P(T > 1/3) = e−3 × 1/3 = 0.3679. Same event, same answer.

(c) Given X > 4, X is uniform on (4, 10), a stretch of length 6, and the part above 7 has length 3: P(X > 7 | X > 4) = 3/6 = 0.5. But P(X > 3) = 7/10 = 0.7. Having waited 4 minutes makes 3 more minutes less likely to be needed: the uniform remembers, because the bus must come by minute 10.

In one or two sentences of your own: why must the waiting time between Poisson events be memoryless?

Compare with a model answer

Because Poisson counts in non-overlapping windows are independent: having seen no call up to time s tells you nothing about the window after s, so the chance of waiting t more is the chance of an empty window of length t, e−λt, whatever s was.

Your turn, half done. Customers arrive Poisson at 12 per hour. Find the mean wait for the next customer, and the chance it is more than 10 minutes.

Step 1: the wait is exponential with rate 12 per hour, so the mean is 1/12 hour = ____ minutes. Step 2: 10 minutes is 1/6 hour, so P(T > 1/6) = e−12 × 1/6 = e−____ = ____ .

Check your steps

The mean wait is 5 minutes. P(T > 10 min) = e^(−2) = 0.1353, the same as P(Poisson(2) = 0), since 10 minutes expects 2 customers.

The uniform is the seed of all simulation. A computer makes uniform numbers between 0 and 1, and one formula turns them into exponential waits.

Feed each uniform number U into −ln(1 − U). The results behave like waits with mean 1:

Uniform UWait = −ln(1 − U)
0.100.11
0.500.69
0.902.30
0.994.61

Most values of U give short waits, and only U near 1 gives a long one. Why does it work? The wait is longer than t exactly when U > 1 − e−t, and that has chance e−t. That is the exponential tail with rate 1. Divide by λ to get rate λ.

The names, and where the exponential goes next

The trick is the probability integral transform in your corpus: if U is uniform, F−1(U) has distribution F. For the exponential, that is −ln(1 − U)/λ.

The exponential is the building block of queueing theory (call centres, network packets), survival analysis (time to death, relapse, churn) and continuous-time Markov chains.

Its constant "hazard rate" λ is the baseline that the Cox model in medical statistics multiplies up and down.

Sums of independent exponentials give the gamma distribution (L14): the wait for the r-th Poisson event. Sums of geometrics gave the negative binomial in just the same way.

Why this matters for you

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

(1) For a waiting time T ≥ 0 with a density, show E[T] = ∫0∞ P(T > t) dt, the continuous twin of L6's tail-sum formula. (2) Use it to get the exponential's mean 1/λ, and its MGF (L11) to get the variance.

Worked proof

(1) Write T as the length of the stretch from 0 to T: T = ∫0∞ 1{t < T} dt, where 1{t < T} is 1 while t is still below T and 0 after. Average both sides; swapping the average and the integral is allowed because everything is non-negative, and the average of the 0/1 indicator is its probability (L6):

E[T] = ∫0∞ P(T > t) dt ∎

(2) For the exponential, ∫0∞ e−λt dt = 1/λ. The MGF:

M(s) = ∫0∞ est λe−λt dt = λ/(λ − s),  for s < λ.

Expand: λ/(λ − s) = 1 + s/λ + s²/λ² + …, so E[T] = 1/λ and E[T²]/2! = 1/λ², giving E[T²] = 2/λ² and Var(T) = 2/λ² − 1/λ² = 1/λ². ∎

An ISI entrance problem

Here is a real ISI question, in plain words first. Two bulbs burn side by side, independently. Each one's life is exponential with mean 2. On average, how long until both have died? The options are (A) 4, (B) 3.5, (C) 3 and (D) 2.5.

Source: ISI M.Stat 2021 objective paper (PSA), Q18. In the paper's notation, X1 and X2 are independent exponentials, each with mean 2. It asks for the expectation of max{X1, X2}.

Worked solution

The maximum is when the second bulb dies. Split that time into two waits.

First wait: until one of them dies. Both survive past t when each does, and they're independent (L11), so

P(min > t) = e−t/2 × e−t/2 = e−t

That's exponential with rate 1 (the two rates of 1/2 add), mean 1.

Second wait: the survivor's remaining life. By memorylessness, the bulb still burning is as good as new. So its remaining life is a fresh exponential with mean 2.

Wait untilIts kindAverage
one bulb diesexponential, rate 1/2 + 1/2 = 11
the survivor diesa fresh exponential, mean 22
both have diedthe maximum1 + 2 = 3

Add the two average waits by linearity: E[max] = 1 + 2 = 3, option (C).

Check with the tail formula. P(max ≤ t) = (1 − e−t/2)², so P(max > t) = 2e−t/2 − e−t, and

E[max] = ∫0∞ (2e−t/2 − e−t) dt = 4 − 1 = 3

Checked by script (practice bank, ISI checks, file psa-2021-q18): a Monte Carlo estimate of E max was 3.0006, against the exact 3. The distractor 4 is the sum of the two means. 2.5 comes from forgetting that the survivor starts afresh.
