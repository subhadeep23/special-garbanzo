1 · Waiting for the first six

Roll a die until a six turns up. Let X be the roll on which the first six appears. Binomial fixed the number of rolls and counted sixes (L8); now the number of sixes is fixed at one and the number of rolls is random.

The first six comes on roll k exactly when the first k − 1 rolls miss and roll k hits. That's a single pattern, MM…MH, so no counting is needed; independence multiplies:

P(X = k) = qk−1 p,  k = 1, 2, 3, …

with p = 1/6 and q = 5/6. This is the geometric distribution: each probability is q times the one before, a geometric sequence. Even simpler, and more useful, is the tail. Needing more than k rolls means the first k all missed:

P(X > k) = qk

Now the mean, with no calculus at all. Step through, and predict first.

The same sum with any p gives 1 + q + q² + … = 1/(1 − q) = 1/p. Rare successes mean long waits: chance 1/6 means 6 rolls on average, chance 1/100 means 100. (It's the same series you summed for the forgetful lock in L6.) The variance is q/p²; Go deeper derives it.

TWO CONVENTIONS  Books disagree on what to count. Trials (this lesson's X): support 1, 2, …, P(X = k) = qk−1p, mean 1/p. Failures before the first success (Y = X − 1; MIT 18.05 and the first form in your corpus): support 0, 1, …, P(Y = y) = qyp, mean 1/p − 1 = q/p. Their variances are equal, q/p², because subtracting 1 doesn't change spread (L7). Always check the support before you use a formula. That single check settles most geometric exam questions.

2 · The die forgets

Suppose three rolls have passed with no six. Does that change the chance of a six on the next roll, or the wait still ahead? Predict, then slide.

In symbols, using L2's conditional probability:

P(X > m + k | X > m) = qm+k/qm = qk = P(X > k)

Say it in one line

The geometric distribution is memoryless: given no success so far, the wait still ahead has the same distribution as a fresh start. Past failures neither bring success nearer nor push it away.

"Five rolls without a six, so a six is due." This is the gambler's fallacy, and memorylessness is its exact refutation. The die has no record of past rolls; each roll is a fresh 1/6. In the picture, after any number of misses the next bar is 1/6 again. The fallacy feels right because a long run of misses is unlikely before it starts: six misses in a row has chance (5/6)6 ≈ 0.335. But once five misses have happened, they're no longer uncertain, and only the next roll is. Its chance is 1/6, whatever came before.

A bus that never learns. Picture a stop where buses come at random with no timetable. Having waited ten minutes tells you nothing about the next minute; your expected wait from now is what it was when you arrived. That is memorylessness: past waiting is a sunk cost, not a down payment.

Where it breaks: real buses run on timetables, so a long wait does make the next bus nearer; their waits are not memoryless. Light bulbs wear out, so an old bulb is more likely to fail soon. Among waits counted in whole tries, only the geometric forgets exactly; your corpus calls this a characterisation. Its continuous cousin is the exponential (L12).

3 · Waiting for the r-th success

Now wait for the third head when tossing a coin. The third head comes on toss k exactly when toss k is a head and the first k − 1 tosses hold exactly 2 heads. Count the patterns for the first part, price one pattern, multiply, as in L8:

P(X = k) = C(k − 1, r − 1) pr qk−r,  k = r, r + 1, …

This is the negative binomial distribution (trials convention). The last trial is always a success, so only r − 1 successes are free to move. Its mean is r/p: the wait for the r-th success is r separate geometric waits laid end to end, each a fresh start, and by linearity (L6) their means add. Binomial fixes the trials and counts successes; negative binomial fixes the successes and counts trials.

4 · Worked example: the first six, and the third head

A fair die is rolled until the first six; X is the roll on which it appears. (a) Find P(X = 3), E[X] and Var(X). (b) Find P(X > 5 | X > 2). (c) A fair coin is tossed until the third head. Find the chance it comes on the fifth toss.

(a) Miss, miss, hit:

P(X = 3) = (5/6)²(1/6) = 25/216 = 0.1157

The mean is 1/p = 6, and the variance q/p² = (5/6)/(1/36) = 30, an SD of about 5.5. Waits are very spread out: long dry spells are common.

(b) Memorylessness: given more than 2 rolls, needing more than 5 is needing more than 3 more:

P(X > 5 | X > 2) = P(X > 3) = (5/6)³ = 125/216 = 0.5787

(c) r = 3, k = 5, p = 1/2. The first 4 tosses must hold exactly 2 heads, C(4, 2) = 6 patterns, and every full pattern has chance (1/2)5:

P(X = 5) = 6 × (1/2)5 = 6/32 = 3/16

In one or two sentences of your own: why does "P(X > k) = qk" make memorylessness almost automatic?

Compare with a model answer

Surviving m + k tries means surviving m and then k more, and with independent tries the chances multiply: qm+k = qm × qk. Dividing by the qm you already survived leaves qk, the same as starting fresh.

Your turn, half done. A die is rolled until the second six. Find the chance it comes on the fourth roll.

Step 1: roll 4 is a six, and the first 3 rolls hold exactly ____ six, in C(3, 1) = ____ patterns. Step 2: each full pattern has chance (1/6)²(5/6)² = ____ /1296. Step 3: multiply.

Check your steps

One six among the first 3 rolls, in 3 patterns; each full pattern has chance 25/1296; so the answer is 3 × 25/1296 = 75/1296 = 25/432 ≈ 0.0579.

Waiting times are everywhere in statistics. Reliability engineering counts cycles until failure; quality control counts items inspected until the first defect. The coupon collector problem, how many cereal boxes until you hold all n toys, is a sum of geometric waits with shrinking p, and its mean is about n ln n. The negative binomial is also the standard model for overdispersed counts, counts whose variance exceeds their mean, such as the mosquito bites and insect eggs your corpus mentions: it is what you get when a Poisson's λ itself varies from unit to unit, the "gusty rain" of L9. In continuous time, memorylessness belongs to the exponential distribution (L12), and together with L9's Poisson process it builds the Markov chains and queues of stochastic processes.

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

Use a "first step" argument to derive the geometric's mean 1/p and variance q/p² (trials convention) without summing any series.

Worked proof

Look at the first try. With chance p it succeeds and X = 1. With chance q it fails, and by memorylessness the wait from then on is a fresh copy X′ of X, so X = 1 + X′. Averaging over the two cases, weighted by their chances (the total probability rule of L3, applied to averages):

E[X] = p · 1 + q · (1 + E[X])

so (1 − q)E[X] = 1 and E[X] = 1/p. For the second moment, square both cases:

E[X²] = p + q(1 + 2E[X] + E[X²])

so pE[X²] = 1 + 2q/p and E[X²] = 1/p + 2q/p². Then

Var(X) = 1/p + 2q/p² − 1/p² = (p + 2q − 1)/p² = q/p² ∎

Your corpus gets the same results by differentiating the series Σ qx = 1/(1 − q); the first-step route is shorter and is exactly what the ISI problem below needs.

An ISI entrance problem

From the ISI JRF in Statistics 2022 (STA), Q8: Player I rolls a die and Player II randomly predicts the outcome. If the actual outcome i differs from the prediction, Player I's score increases by i and the die is rolled again. This continues until Player II predicts correctly; then nothing is added and the score is final, S. Find the expectation and variance of S.

Worked solution

Each round, the prediction is right with chance 1/6 (a random guess at a fair die). Given the guess was wrong, the outcome is still equally likely to be any of 1 to 6: each outcome i is ruled wrong by 5 of the 6 guesses, the same for every i. So a wrong round adds a fair-die score D with E[D] = 3.5 and E[D²] = 91/6.

First step. With chance 1/6 the game ends now and S = 0. With chance 5/6 it adds D and starts afresh (memoryless!), so S = D + S′ with S′ a fresh copy independent of D:

E[S] = (5/6)(3.5 + E[S])  ⇒  E[S]/6 = 35/12  ⇒  E[S] = 17.5

Squaring the second case (D and S′ are independent, so the middle average factorises):

E[(D + S′)²] = E[D²] + 2E[D]E[S] + E[S²]

so

E[S²] = (5/6)(91/6 + 2(3.5)(17.5) + E[S²])

so E[S²] = 5(91/6 + 122.5) = 4130/6, and

Var(S) = 4130/6 − 17.5² = 4585/12 ≈ 382.08

Sanity check on the mean: there are on average q/p = 5 wrong rounds before the right one (failures convention), each worth 3.5 on average: 5 × 3.5 = 17.5. ✓

Checked by script (the practice bank's ISI checks, file jrf-sta-2022-q8): a million simulated games gave E S ≈ 17.5 and Var S within 1% of 4585/12, and enumeration confirms the outcome is uniform given a wrong guess. The step "E[DS′] = E[D]E[S′] for independent D, S′" is proved in L15; here it is used once.
