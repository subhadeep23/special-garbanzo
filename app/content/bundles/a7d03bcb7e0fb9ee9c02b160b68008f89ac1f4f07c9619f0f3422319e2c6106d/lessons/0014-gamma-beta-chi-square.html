1 · Waiting for the r-th call

Calls arrive Poisson-style at λ per hour. L12 timed the wait for the first call: exponential with rate λ. Now wait for the third. By memorylessness the wait splits into three fresh exponential waits laid end to end (first call, then the second, then the third), all independent. So the total is a sum, and L11 says its MGF is the product:

M(t) = (λ/(λ − t))r,  t < λ.

That's the barcode of the gamma distribution with shape r and rate λ, whose density is

f(t) = λr tr−1 e−λt/Γ(r),  t > 0

where Γ(r) = (r − 1)! for whole r (the gamma function, Γ(p) = ∫0∞ e−uup−1 du, extends it to any p > 0, with Γ(1/2) = √π). Means and variances of the r waits add: E[T] = r/λ, Var(T) = r/λ². With r = 1 it's the exponential again.

And here's the most useful fact about it, no integral required: the r-th call comes after time t exactly when fewer than r calls have arrived by t. Pick r and t, and predict first.

Say it in one line

The gamma is the wait for the r-th Poisson event: a sum of r exponentials, mean r/λ, and its tail is a Poisson sum, P(Tr > t) = P(Poisson(λt) ≤ r − 1).

A relay race. Each runner's leg is an exponential wait; the team's total time is the gamma. Add runners (raise r) and the total gets longer and more spread out, but relatively less erratic: its SD grows like √r while its mean grows like r. Adding gammas with the same rate is just merging teams, which is why, for independent gammas,

Gamma(r1, λ) + Gamma(r2, λ) = Gamma(r1 + r2, λ)

Where it breaks: the shape need not be a whole number. Gamma(2.5, λ) is a perfectly good distribution with no "two and a half runners" story; it's defined by its density and the gamma function. And teams only merge cleanly when the runners share one rate.

"Gamma(2, 3) has mean 6." Maybe, maybe not: it depends on whether 3 is a scale or a rate. With shape 2 and scale 3 (density ∝ t e−t/3) the mean is 6; with shape 2 and rate 3 (∝ t e−3t) it's 2/3. Your corpus writes G(α, p) with density ∝ e−αxxp−1, so α is a rate and p the shape; elsewhere it uses θ as a scale. Before using any gamma formula, look at the exponent: e−(rate)×x or e−x/(scale). Mean = shape/rate = shape × scale.

WATCH OUT  Two confirmed slips in your corpus's gamma pages (both in the scanned notes, not OCR). (1) "γ(α, 1) is an exponential distribution with mean α": with density αe−αx, α is the rate, so the mean is 1/α. (2) "Problem 2: harmonic mean of the geometric distribution" is about the gamma, and its answer α/(p − 1) is E[1/X], not the harmonic mean. The HM is 1/E[1/X] = (p − 1)/α, for p > 1. That makes seven and eight confirmed slips in this corpus.

2 · Chi-square: squared errors are gammas

Square a standard normal (L13). Its MGF takes one line, by the same completing-the-square as L13's Go deeper:

E[etZ²] = ∫ e−(1−2t)z²/2/√(2π) dz = (1 − 2t)−1/2,  t < 1/2.

Compare (λ/(λ − t))r with rate λ = 1/2 and shape r = 1/2: the same barcode. Add n independent squares and the MGFs multiply to (1 − 2t)−n/2. The sum Z1² + … + Zn² has the chi-square distribution with n degrees of freedom, written χ²ₙ, which is Gamma(n/2, rate 1/2). Its mean is (n/2)/(1/2) = n (each square averages E[Z²] = 1) and its variance (n/2)/(1/4) = 2n. Independent χ²s add their degrees of freedom, like merging relay teams.

3 · Beta: shares of a whole

Some quantities live between 0 and 1: a proportion, a probability, "what fraction of the way". The beta distribution Beta(a, b) on (0, 1) has density proportional to xa−1(1 − x)b−1, mean a/(a + b), and variance ab/((a + b)²(a + b + 1)). With a = b = 1 it's the uniform (L12). It turns up in two natural ways:

1Order statistics. The larger of two independent U(0, 1) numbers is at most x when both are: P(max ≤ x) = x², so its density is 2x, which is Beta(2, 1). In general the k-th smallest of n uniforms is Beta(k, n − k + 1).
2Shares of gamma totals. If X ~ Gamma(a, λ) and Y ~ Gamma(b, λ) are independent, then X/(X + Y) ~ Beta(a, b): the first team's share of the total race time. (The proof is a change of variables, L16.)

4 · Worked examples

(a) Calls arrive at 2 per hour. T is the time until the 3rd call. Find E[T] and P(T > 1 hour). (b) Find the mean and variance of χ²₁₀. (c) X ~ Beta(2, 3). Find E[X] and Var(X).

(a) T ~ Gamma(3, rate 2), so E[T] = 3/2 hour. The 3rd call comes after an hour exactly when the first hour holds at most 2 calls, and that count is Poisson(2):

P(T > 1) = e−2(1 + 2 + 2²/2) = 5e−2 = 0.6767

No integral of the gamma density needed.

(b) χ²₁₀ is Gamma(5, rate 1/2): mean n = 10, variance 2n = 20.

(c) E[X] = 2/(2 + 3) = 0.4, and

Var(X) = (2 × 3)/(5² × 6) = 6/150 = 0.04

In one or two sentences of your own: why is "the r-th call comes after t" the same event as "fewer than r calls by t"?

Compare with a model answer

If at least r calls had arrived by t, the r-th would already have come, by t at the latest. So the r-th call is still to come after t exactly when the count by t is at most r − 1: the two statements describe the same outcomes.

Your turn, half done. Buses arrive Poisson at 1 per hour. Find the chance you're still waiting for the 2nd bus after an hour.

Step 1: still waiting means at most ____ bus in the first hour. Step 2: that count is Poisson(____), so the chance is e−1(1 + 1) = ____ .

Check your steps

At most 1 bus; the count is Poisson(1); so the chance is 2e^(−1) ≈ 0.7358. As a gamma: T ~ Gamma(2, rate 1), mean 2 hours.

This family carries most of classical inference. Here is the beta at work: learning a coin's unknown chance of heads, p.

StageBelief about pIts mean, a/(a + b)
before any tosses: p spread evenly from 0 to 1Beta(1, 1)1/2
after 7 heads and 3 tailsBeta(1 + 7, 1 + 3) = Beta(8, 4)8/12 = 2/3

The data just add to the two numbers: heads to the first, tails to the second. That is the L3 Bayes machine, running on whole distributions.

The χ² does a different job. A normal sample's spread, (n − 1)S²/σ², is χ² with n − 1 degrees of freedom (Unit III).

The names, and where the family goes next

That χ² fact is where every variance confidence interval comes from. Ratios of χ²s give the t and F distributions behind t-tests, regression and ANOVA. Pearson's χ² goodness-of-fit test is named for it.

In Bayesian statistics, the beta is the natural prior for a binomial p, and the gamma for a Poisson λ. These are "conjugate priors": after the data, the posterior stays in the same family, with updated parameters.

Queueing theory calls the whole-number gamma the Erlang distribution. Order statistics of uniforms are betas, and they underlie the nonparametric tests of Unit V.

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

Show that P(T > t) = P(Poisson(λt) ≤ r − 1) for T ~ Gamma(r, λ) with whole r, directly from the density, by differentiating the right-hand side.

Worked proof

Let G(t) = e−λt Σk=0r−1 (λt)k/k!. Differentiate term by term. Each term e−λt(λt)k/k! has derivative

λe−λt[(λt)k−1/(k − 1)! − (λt)k/k!]

(the first part is absent when k = 0). Summed over k = 0 … r − 1, the bracket telescopes, leaving only −(λt)r−1/(r − 1)!. So

G′(t) = −λrtr−1e−λt/(r − 1)! = −f(t)

And P(T > t) also has derivative −f(t). The two functions have the same slope everywhere and both equal 1 at t = 0, so they're equal. ∎ Your corpus reaches the same identity by integrating by parts r − 1 times.

An ISI entrance problem

Here is a real ISI question, in plain words first. Add up n independent copies of the same random number X. You're told the total is exponential with mean n. Must each copy then be exponential with mean 1? Say true or false, with reasons. (L11 solved part (b), the binomial version.)

Source: ISI M.Stat 2020 descriptive paper (PSB), Q4(a). In the paper's notation, X1, …, Xn are independent and identically distributed, and Sn = X1 + … + Xn.

Worked solution

False. Let M be the common MGF. Independence gives M(t)n = MSn(t) = (1 − nt)−1, the MGF of an exponential with mean n (rate 1/n). M is positive, so take the positive n-th root:

M(t) = (1 − nt)−1/n = ((1/n)/((1/n) − t))1/n

That's the Gamma(shape 1/n, rate 1/n) barcode. So by uniqueness, each Xi is Gamma(1/n, 1/n).

The claim needsXi really is
exponential, mean 1Gamma(1/n, 1/n)
mean 1mean (1/n)/(1/n) = 1
MGF (1 − t)−1MGF (1 − nt)−1/n

The means agree, but the distributions don't, unless n = 1. Teams of n "fractional runners", each with shape 1/n, merge into one full exponential leg.

The lesson: matching the mean isn't matching the distribution. Contrast part (b), where a binomial's n-th root is a binomial.

Checked by script (practice bank, ISI checks, file psb-2020-q4). Summing 4 independent Gamma(¼, scale 4) variables reproduced the Exp(mean 4) quantiles to within 2%. A single one has P(X < 0.01) ≈ 0.25, against 0.01 for an exponential with mean 1.
