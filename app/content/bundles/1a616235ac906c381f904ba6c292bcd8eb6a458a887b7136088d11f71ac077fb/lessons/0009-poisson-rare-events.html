Poisson — Counting Rare Events

Many tiny chances, one count, one number λ. ~20 min.

TODAY'S WIN  You'll be able to use Poisson(λ) for counts of rare events in a window, scale λ to the window you're asked about, use it as a stand-in for Bin(n, p) when n is large and p is small, and recognise it by its signature: mean = variance = λ.

• A binomial count has mean 4 and variance 3. What are n and p?

1 · Many tiny chances

Think of raindrops landing on one paving stone during a one-minute shower. Split the minute into thousands of tiny instants. In each instant a drop hits the stone or it doesn't, with a tiny chance; instants don't influence each other. You don't care when the drops hit, only how many.

That's a binomial with a huge n and a tiny p (L8). But you never know n or p separately; nobody counts instants. What you can know is the average count, say 2 drops a minute. So ask: what happens to Bin(n, p) when n grows, p shrinks, and the mean np = 2 stays fixed? Predict first.

The limit has a formula. Write p = λ/n in the binomial and let n grow. The chance of no events, (1 − λ/n)n, tends to e−λ (a standard limit; the Go-deeper block shows the rest). What survives is the Poisson distribution with mean λ:

P(X = x) = e−λ λx/x!,  x = 0, 1, 2, …

Two checks it's a real mass function: every term is positive, and, using the series for eλ, they add to

e−λ × (1 + λ + λ²/2! + …) = e−λ eλ = 1

There's no upper limit on x: in principle any number of drops could fall.

Say it in one line

A count of many independent, individually rare events is Poisson(λ), where λ is the average count in the window, and its mean and its variance are both λ.

Why both? The binomial's mean is np = λ, and its variance is npq = λ(1 − p), which tends to λ as p shrinks. (Directly from the formula too: see Go deeper.)

Raindrops on a paving stone. Each instant is a tiny Bernoulli trial; the count of drops in the minute is Poisson. Typos on a page, calls to a help line in an hour, decays from a radioactive sample in a second, and defects in a metre of cloth all fit the same picture: lots of opportunities, each very unlikely, independent of each other.

Where it breaks: real rain comes in gusts, and gusts make drops arrive together. Then the instants aren't independent, the counts clump, and the variance comes out bigger than the mean ("overdispersion"). Your corpus gives the same warning about insect deaths that cluster, and about a book's first proof, where misprints aren't rare enough.

2 · λ belongs to the window

A Poisson count always refers to a window: a minute, a page, a metre. If events arrive at an average rate per unit, λ is the rate times the window's length. Predict before step 2.

"Poisson approximates any binomial with a large n." No: it needs a small p as well, so that each trial is rare. Take Bin(100, 1/2): mean 50, variance 100 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 25. Poisson(50) has the same mean but variance 50, twice as wide, so it's a poor stand-in. The test is in the variances: the binomial's is λ(1 − p), the Poisson's is λ, and they agree only when p is small. Note what is not required: a large λ. Poisson works for λ = 0.5 just as well as for λ = 5.

3 · Worked examples

(a) X is Poisson with P(X = 1) = P(X = 2). Find P(X = 0). (b) A batch of 100 items has a defect rate of 2%. Find the chance of no defects exactly, and by the Poisson approximation. (c) A help line gets 3 calls an hour on average. What is the chance of at least one call in a 30-minute break?

(a) Set the two formulas equal; the e−λ cancels: λ = λ²/2. Since λ > 0, λ = 2. Then

P(X = 0) = e−2 = 0.1353

A handy shortcut behind this: P(X = x)/P(X = x − 1) = λ/x. Neighbouring probabilities are equal exactly when x = λ, so for whole-number λ the two most likely counts are λ and λ − 1.

(b) Exactly, the defects are Bin(100, 0.02):

P(no defect) = 0.98100 = 0.1326

Poisson with λ = np = 2 gives e−2 = 0.1353. The gap is 0.0027: n is large and p small, so the stand-in is good.

(c) Scale the rate to the window: λ = 3 × 1/2 = 1.5. "At least one" is the complement of "none":

P(X ≥ 1) = 1 − e−1.5 = 0.7769

In one or two sentences of your own: why does a Poisson count need only one number, λ, while a binomial needs two?

Compare with a model answer

In the limit, n and p matter only through their product np: making the instants finer multiplies n and divides p by the same factor and changes nothing you can observe. So the single surviving number is the mean count λ = np.

Your turn, half done. Typos occur at an average of 1 per 2 pages. Find the chance a 4-page letter has no typos.

Step 1: rate × window: λ = (1/2) × 4 = ____ . Step 2: P(X = 0) = e−λ = ____ (to 4 places).

Check your steps

λ = 2, so P(X = 0) = e^(−2) = 0.1353. Same number as example (a), from a completely different story.

The Poisson is the basic model for counts everywhere: insurance claims, web requests, mutations along a DNA strand, goals in a football match. Ladislaus Bortkiewicz's famous 1898 data set, deaths from horse kicks in the Prussian cavalry, fits it closely. The same assumptions in continuous time give the Poisson process: counts in non-overlapping windows are independent, and the waiting time between events turns out to be exponential (L12). In regression, counts are modelled with Poisson regression; when the data show variance well above the mean, statisticians switch to the negative binomial (L10), which is exactly the "gusty rain" fix. And the "mean = variance" signature gives a quick diagnostic for whether count data could be Poisson at all.

Why this matters for you

EXAM  Expect: "P(X = 1) = P(X = 2), find λ, then a probability"; rescaling λ to a new window; "mean = variance" to identify a Poisson; the approximation to Bin(n, p) with λ = np; the mode (λ and λ − 1 when λ is whole); and factorial moments, E[X(X − 1)…(X − r + 1)] = λr. The sum of independent Poissons is Poisson too; you'll prove it with MGFs in L11.

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

(1) Show that Bin(n, λ/n) probabilities tend to the Poisson formula as n grows. (2) Show that every factorial moment of Poisson(λ) is a power of λ, E[X(X − 1)…(X − r + 1)] = λr, and deduce mean = variance = λ.

Worked proof

(1) Fix x and write the binomial with p = λ/n, splitting it into four factors:

C(n, x)(λ/n)x(1 − λ/n)n−x

= [n(n − 1)…(n − x + 1)/nx] · λx/x! · (1 − λ/n)n · (1 − λ/n)−x

The first factor is 1(1 − 1/n)…(1 − (x − 1)/n), a fixed number of terms each tending to 1. The last factor also tends to 1, since x is fixed. The third tends to e−λ, the standard limit (1 + a/n)n → ea. What's left is e−λλx/x!. ∎

(2) The terms with x < r vanish (one factor is 0). For x ≥ r, the factor x(x − 1)…(x − r + 1) cancels the top of x!, leaving (x − r)!:

E[X(X − 1)…(X − r + 1)] = e−λλr Σx ≥ r λx−r/(x − r)! = λr

because the last sum is the series for eλ. With r = 1: E[X] = λ. With r = 2: E[X(X − 1)] = λ², so E[X²] = λ² + λ and Var(X) = λ² + λ − λ² = λ. ∎

An ISI entrance problem

From the ISI M.Stat 2022 objective paper (PSA), Q18: X follows a Poisson distribution whose mass function f satisfies f(3) = f(4). What is E[X(X − 1)(X − 2)]? Options: (A) 27, (B) 3, (C) 4, (D) 64.

Worked solution

Use the ratio of neighbours: f(4)/f(3) = λ/4. Equal neighbours means the ratio is 1, so λ = 4.

The quantity asked for is the third factorial moment, which you just proved equals λ3:

E[X(X − 1)(X − 2)] = 43 = 64,  option (D).

The distractor 27 is 33, from mistaking λ for 3, the smaller of the two tied counts.

Checked by script (the practice bank's ISI checks, file psa-2022-q18): f(3) = f(4) = 0.195367 at λ = 4, and the sum of x(x − 1)(x − 2)f(x) over x up to 119 is 64.000000. That script used to crash before printing its verdict (a float overflow past x = 170); it was fixed while writing this lesson.
