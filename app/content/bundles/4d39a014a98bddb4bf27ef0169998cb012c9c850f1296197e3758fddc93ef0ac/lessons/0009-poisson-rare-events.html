1 · A thousand scratch cards

The stall owner has a box of 1,000 scratch cards. Each one wins with chance 1 in 1,000, and the cards don't affect each other.

"Bet ₹10 that at least one card in the box is a winner," he says. "If there's a winner, I pay you ₹12."

Your puzzle · 3 minutes, a phone calculator helps
  1. What is the chance one particular card loses?
  2. The cards are independent. What is the chance all 1,000 lose? (Multiply, L2.) Type 0.999^1000 into your phone.
  3. So what is the chance of at least one winner?

Voice A: "1,000 cards, each 1/1000. Add them up: 1000 × 1/1000 = 1. A winner is certain. Easy money."

Voice B: "L1's adding rule only works when things can't happen together. Two cards can both win. So you can't just add."

Write down the chance you got, and whether ₹12 back for ₹10 is a good deal.

2 · What you already know: the number e

Your answer, about 0.368, is a famous number in disguise. You met it in school through interest.

Put ₹1 in a bank paying 100% a year. Paid once a year, you get ₹2. Paid twice, each half-year adds 50%: 1.5² = 2.25. Paid daily: (1 + 1/365)365 = 2.7146. Paid every instant, the total settles at e = 2.71828…

Now run it backwards. Lose a tiny 1/n of what you have, n times over: (1 − 1/n)n settles at 1/e = e−1 = 0.3679. That's your scratch-card answer: 1,000 tiny chances to lose, each taking 1/1000 off. And if each card had chance λ/n, the same limit gives e−λ.

Voice B wins. The chance of at least one winner is about 1 − 0.368 = 0.632, not 1. You'd win ₹12 about 63 times in 100 bets and lose ₹10 about 37 times: about ₹390 ahead per 100 bets. Take it, but it's far from certain.

3 · Many tiny chances

Think of raindrops landing on one paving stone during a one-minute shower. Split the minute into thousands of tiny instants. In each instant a drop hits the stone or it doesn't, with a tiny chance; instants don't influence each other. You don't care when the drops hit, only how many.

That's a binomial with a huge n and a tiny p (L8). But you never know n or p separately; nobody counts instants. What you can know is the average count, say 2 drops a minute. So ask: what happens to Bin(n, p) when n grows, p shrinks, and the mean np = 2 stays fixed? Predict first.

The limit has a formula. Write p = λ/n in the binomial and let n grow. The chance of no events, (1 − λ/n)n, tends to e−λ, the "lose a tiny bit many times" limit from section 2. (The Go-deeper block shows the rest.) What survives is the Poisson distribution with mean λ:

P(X = x) = e−λ λx/x!,  x = 0, 1, 2, …

Two checks it's a real mass function: every term is positive, and, using the series for eλ, they add to

e−λ × (1 + λ + λ²/2! + …) = e−λ eλ = 1

There's no upper limit on x: in principle any number of drops could fall.

Say it in one line

A count of many independent, individually rare events is Poisson(λ), where λ is the average count in the window, and its mean and its variance are both λ.

Why both? The binomial's mean is np = λ, and its variance is npq = λ(1 − p), which tends to λ as p shrinks. (Directly from the formula too: see Go deeper.)

Typos on a page, calls to a help line in an hour, and defects in a metre of cloth fit the same picture: lots of chances, each very unlikely, none affecting the others. So do the scratch cards: 1,000 cards, λ = 1.

Where it breaks: real rain comes in gusts, and gusts make drops arrive together. Then the instants aren't independent, the counts clump, and the variance comes out bigger than the mean ("overdispersion"). Your corpus gives the same warning about insect deaths that cluster, and about a book's first proof, where misprints aren't rare enough.

4 · λ belongs to the window

A Poisson count always refers to a window: a minute, a page, a metre. If events arrive at an average rate per unit, λ is the rate times the window's length. Predict before step 2.

"Poisson approximates any binomial with a large n." No: it needs a small p as well, so that each trial is rare. Take Bin(100, 1/2): mean 50, variance 100 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 25. Poisson(50) has the same mean but variance 50, twice as wide, so it's a poor stand-in. The test is in the variances: the binomial's is λ(1 − p), the Poisson's is λ, and they agree only when p is small. Note what is not required: a large λ. Poisson works for λ = 0.5 just as well as for λ = 5.

5 · Worked examples

(a) X is Poisson with P(X = 1) = P(X = 2). Find P(X = 0). (b) A batch of 100 items has a defect rate of 2%. Find the chance of no defects exactly, and by the Poisson approximation. (c) A help line gets 3 calls an hour on average. What is the chance of at least one call in a 30-minute break?

(a) Set the two formulas equal; the e−λ cancels: λ = λ²/2. Since λ > 0, λ = 2. Then

P(X = 0) = e−2 = 0.1353

A handy shortcut behind this: P(X = x)/P(X = x − 1) = λ/x. Neighbouring probabilities are equal exactly when x = λ, so for whole-number λ the two most likely counts are λ and λ − 1.

(b) Exactly, the defects are Bin(100, 0.02):

P(no defect) = 0.98100 = 0.1326

Poisson with λ = np = 2 gives e−2 = 0.1353. The gap is 0.0027: n is large and p small, so the stand-in is good.

(c) Scale the rate to the window: λ = 3 × 1/2 = 1.5. "At least one" is the complement of "none":

P(X ≥ 1) = 1 − e−1.5 = 0.7769

In one or two sentences of your own: why does a Poisson count need only one number, λ, while a binomial needs two?

Compare with a model answer

In the limit, n and p matter only through their product np: making the instants finer multiplies n and divides p by the same factor and changes nothing you can observe. So the single surviving number is the mean count λ = np.

Your pocket question · carry it for a few days

Buses come at random, with no timetable: on average one every 10 minutes, the Poisson way. You walk to the stop at a random moment. How long do you wait, on average? Most people say 5 minutes, half the gap. Is that right?

Hint 1 (after a day)

When you turn up at a random moment, are you more likely to land inside a long gap between buses or a short one?

Hint 2 (after another day)

Try a simpler case. Gaps are 5 minutes or 15 minutes, equally often, so the average gap is 10. What share of all the time is spent inside 15-minute gaps? What is your average wait then?

The answer (try for three days first)

Long gaps take up more of the clock, so you tend to land in them. In the 5-or-15 case, 3/4 of the time is inside 15-minute gaps, and the average wait is (1/4)(2.5) + (3/4)(7.5) = 6.25 minutes, already more than 5. For truly random Poisson buses it's worse: the average wait is the full 10 minutes. The bus has no memory of when the last one came. That "no memory" idea is the star of L10 and L12. This is called the inspection paradox.

The Poisson is the basic model for counts everywhere: insurance claims, web requests, goals in a football match. Its most famous fit is a grim one.

In 1898, Ladislaus Bortkiewicz counted deaths from horse kicks in the Prussian cavalry, one army corps and one year at a time. Over 200 corps-years there were 0.61 deaths a year on average. Here is what a Poisson with mean 0.61 predicts:

Where the Poisson goes next

The same assumptions in continuous time give the Poisson process. Counts in windows that don't overlap are independent. The waiting time between events turns out to be exponential (L12).

In regression, counts are modelled with Poisson regression.

Sometimes the data's variance is well above its mean. Then statisticians switch to the negative binomial (L10), which is exactly the "gusty rain" fix.

The "mean = variance" signature also gives a quick check of whether count data could be Poisson at all.

Why this matters for you

EXAM  Expect "P(X = 1) = P(X = 2), find λ, then a probability". Expect rescaling λ to a new window, "mean = variance" to identify a Poisson, and the stand-in for Bin(n, p) with λ = np.

Also: the most likely counts (λ and λ − 1 when λ is whole), and factorial moments, E[X(X − 1)…(X − r + 1)] = λr. The sum of independent Poissons is Poisson too; you'll prove it with MGFs in L11.

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

(1) Show that Bin(n, λ/n) probabilities tend to the Poisson formula as n grows. (2) Show that every factorial moment of Poisson(λ) is a power of λ, E[X(X − 1)…(X − r + 1)] = λr, and deduce mean = variance = λ.

Worked proof

(1) Fix x and write the binomial with p = λ/n, splitting it into four factors:

C(n, x)(λ/n)x(1 − λ/n)n−x

= [n(n − 1)…(n − x + 1)/nx] · λx/x! · (1 − λ/n)n · (1 − λ/n)−x

The first factor is 1(1 − 1/n)…(1 − (x − 1)/n), a fixed number of terms each tending to 1. The last factor also tends to 1, since x is fixed. The third tends to e−λ, the standard limit (1 + a/n)n → ea. What's left is e−λλx/x!. ∎

(2) The terms with x < r vanish (one factor is 0). For x ≥ r, the factor x(x − 1)…(x − r + 1) cancels the top of x!, leaving (x − r)!:

E[X(X − 1)…(X − r + 1)] = e−λλr Σx ≥ r λx−r/(x − r)! = λr

because the last sum is the series for eλ. With r = 1: E[X] = λ. With r = 2: E[X(X − 1)] = λ², so E[X²] = λ² + λ and Var(X) = λ² + λ − λ² = λ. ∎

An ISI entrance problem

Here is a real ISI question, in plain words first. A count X follows a Poisson distribution, and the chances of exactly 3 and exactly 4 are equal. What is the average of X(X − 1)(X − 2)? The options are (A) 27, (B) 3, (C) 4 and (D) 64.

Source: ISI M.Stat 2022 objective paper (PSA), Q18. In the paper's notation, the mass function f satisfies f(3) = f(4), and the question asks for E[X(X − 1)(X − 2)].

Worked solution

Use the ratio of neighbours: f(4)/f(3) = λ/4. Equal neighbours means the ratio is 1, so λ = 4.

kf(k) when λ = 4
20.1465
30.1954
40.1954
50.1563

The quantity asked for is the third factorial moment. You just proved it equals λ3:

E[X(X − 1)(X − 2)] = 43 = 64,  option (D).

The distractor 27 is 33, from mistaking λ for 3, the smaller of the two tied counts.
