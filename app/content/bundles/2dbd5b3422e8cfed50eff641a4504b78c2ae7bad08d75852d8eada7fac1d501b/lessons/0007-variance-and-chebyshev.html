Variance and Chebyshev — How Spread Out?

Two games, the same average, completely different feel. ~20 min.

TODAY'S WIN  You'll measure spread with the variance, rescale it in one line, and use Chebyshev to promise something about any shape.

0 · Spaced recall — no peeking

• P(X = x) = x/10 for x = 1, 2, 3, 4. What were E[X] and E[X²]?

• Discrete X has F(5) = 0.9 and F(5−) = 0.6. What is P(X = 5)?

Answers: 3 and 10, and 10 is bigger than 3² = 9 (L6). And 0.3, the jump at 5 (L4–L5). That gap of 1 between 10 and 9 is today's main character.

1 · Two games with the same price

The stall owner has learned. His new games are both fair: each pays ₹3 a play on average, and each costs ₹3.

AYou always get exactly ₹3.
BA coin decides: ₹1 or ₹5.

Same mean, same price. Yet they feel completely different. A is boring. B is a small thrill every time. The mean from L6 can't tell them apart. We need a second number: how far from the mean does X usually land?

Your puzzle · invent the number · 3 minutes

Invent a number that is 0 for game A and bigger for game B. Small steps:

  1. For game B, how far is each payout from the mean, 3? Keep the sign: below is minus, above is plus.
  2. Average those two distances, using the chances. What do you get? Why is that useless?
  3. How could you stop the pluses and minuses cancelling?

Two voices have a fix each.

Voice A: "Drop the signs. Average the plain distances. Game B gives 2. Simple, and in rupees."

Voice B: "Square the distances, then average. Squares are never negative either. Game B gives 4."

Both stop the cancelling. Which would you pick, and why? Write it down.

2 · What you already know: the gap from L6

In L6 you squared a coin-chosen side of a land plot, 1 m or 3 m. The average area was 5 m², but the square of the average side was only 4 m². That gap of 1 came from the spread: the sides sat 1 m either side of their mean.

Voice B's number is exactly that gap. Statistics picked Voice B. The variance of X is the average squared distance from the mean:

Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²],  where μ = E[X].

It's a weighted average, just like L6. Each squared distance (x − μ)² counts with its chance. Game A: every distance is 0, so the variance is 0. Game B: every distance is 2, so the variance is 2² = 4.

Voice A wasn't wrong. Its number exists, the mean absolute distance. But squares win for one big reason: they give a shortcut, and that shortcut connects back to L6. Your pocket question at the end shows what each choice is secretly best at.

Squaring changes the units: rupees became rupees². To get back to rupees, take the square root. That's the standard deviation, σ = √Var(X): a typical distance from the mean, in the units of X. Game B's is 2.

3 · The shortcut you'll use every time

Open up the square, one piece at a time. (X − μ)² = X² − 2μX + μ². Now take the mean of each piece, using L6's linearity. μ is just a fixed number, so E[2μX] = 2μE[X] = 2μ²:

E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − 2μ² + μ² = E[X²] − μ²

Say it in one line

Variance = mean of the square, minus square of the mean: Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])².

It's the L6 gap, and it's never negative, because it is an average of squares.

L6's weights x/10 had E[X²] = 10 and E[X] = 3. So Var(X) = 10 − 9 = 1 and σ = 1. The mystery gap from last lesson was the variance all along.

"Mean of the square, minus square of the mean: never below zero, the gap in between." The order is the one thing people swap. Swap it and you get 9 − 10 = −1, an impossible negative variance. So the rhyme carries its own check.

4 · Shift it, stretch it

Think of exam marks. Add 5 grace marks to everyone: the class moves up, but nobody gets closer to anyone else. Double everyone's marks: every gap between two students doubles. What does that do to the variance? Predict before step 3.

Var(aX + b) = a² Var(X)  and  SD(aX + b) = |a| SD(X)

The b drops out because sliding changes no distance. The a comes out squared because variance measures squared distances. That's also why a negative a still gives a positive variance: flipping the marks upside down doesn't make the class less spread out.

5 · Chebyshev: a promise for any shape

Two batsmen, both averaging 40. One scores between 35 and 45 nearly every match. The other makes ducks and centuries. Same average, very different players. The selectors want a second number, and it's the standard deviation.

In cricketIn probability
batting averagethe mean μ
one innings minus the averagea distance x − μ
a typical missthe SD σ
a consistent batsmana small variance

Now say a selector knows only a batsman's average and SD, nothing else. Can they promise anything? Yes, and that promise is today's last big idea.

Where it breaks: the SD punishes a century exactly as much as a duck. In cricket a century is good news. Variance doesn't care which side the surprise is on. And the selector's promise is a worst case, not a forecast.

Here is the promise, called Chebyshev's inequality. For any X with mean μ and SD σ, and any k > 0:

P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k²

In words: the chance of landing k or more SDs from the mean is at most 1/k². At most a quarter of the innings lie 2 SDs or more from the average. At most a ninth lie 3 or more away. Any batsman, any shape. Flip it and you get the same thing as a floor: P(|X − μ| < kσ) ≥ 1 − 1/k². Try to break it.

Why must it be true? Say a share q of the probability sits k SDs or more away. Each bit of it adds at least (kσ)² to the variance's weighted sum. So the variance is at least q · k²σ². But the variance is exactly σ². So q can't be more than 1/k². Too much weight far away would make the variance too big. (Go deeper writes this out properly.)

"Chebyshev says about 25% lies beyond 2 SDs." No. It says at most 25%, for the worst possible shape. It's a ceiling, not an estimate.

In the picture, the bell has only 1/8 beyond 2 SDs, and twin peaks has none. Only the strange "extreme" shape touches the ceiling. For the normal curve in L13, the true figure is under 5%. Use Chebyshev when you don't know the shape and need a guarantee. Never quote it as the likely value. It needs no bell shape, no mirror-image shape, no big sample: only a mean and a variance that exist.

WATCH OUT  Your corpus proves Chebyshev from Markov's inequality, P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X]/a, and says Markov holds "for any non-zero quantity a". Wrong: Markov needs X ≥ 0 and a > 0. Try X = −10 or 1, each with chance 1/2. Then E[X] = −4.5, but P(X ≥ 1) = 1/2, which is not below −4.5. The Chebyshev proof is still safe: it uses Markov on (X − μ)², which is never negative. That's the fifth confirmed slip in this corpus, and the scanned page says the same, so it's not an OCR error.

6 · Worked examples: the promise in action

(a) For L6's X (P(X = x) = x/10 on 1, 2, 3, 4), find the variance two ways, then compare Chebyshev's bound for P(|X − 3| ≥ 2) with the truth. (b) A variable has mean 12 and variance 9. Give a lower bound for P(6 < X < 18).

(a) The shortcut first: Var(X) = 10 − 3² = 1, so σ = 1.

The long way, as a check. The distances from 3 are −2, −1, 0, 1. Square each, weight it, add:

Var(X) = 4(0.1) + 1(0.2) + 0(0.3) + 1(0.4) = 1

Chebyshev. A distance of 2 is 2σ, so k = 2 and P(|X − 3| ≥ 2) ≤ 1/2² = 1/4.

The truth. Being 2 or more from 3 happens only at x = 1, since x = 5 isn't possible. So the true chance is P(X = 1) = 1/10. The promise holds, with room to spare: 1/10 against 1/4.

(b) Here μ = 12 and σ = √9 = 3. "Between 6 and 18" means "less than 6 from 12", and 6 = 2σ. So k = 2:

P(6 < X < 18) ≥ 1 − 1/2² = 1 − 9/36 = 3/4

Many exam keys write it as 1 − σ²/c² with c = 6: the same number. Notice what was not given: the shape. That's exactly when Chebyshev is the right tool.

In one or two sentences of your own: why is Chebyshev's bound usually far above the true chance, and when is it exactly right?

Compare with a model answer

It must hold for every shape with that mean and SD, so it is set by the worst case: a shape with all its far weight exactly k SDs out and the rest at the mean. Ordinary shapes spread their weight more evenly and sit well under the ceiling.

Your turn, half done. Exam scores have mean 50 and SD 5, shape unknown. Find the narrowest band centred on 50 that Chebyshev promises holds at least 96% of scores.

Step 1: we need 1 − 1/k² = 0.96, so 1/k² = ____ and k = ____ . Step 2: the band is 50 ± k × 5, which is ____ to ____ .

Check your steps

1/k² = 0.04, so k² = 25 and k = 5. The interval is 50 ± 25, from 25 to 75. A promise for any shape is expensive: it needs a band 5 SDs wide.

Your pocket question · carry it for a few days

Back to the two voices. Pick any number c as your "centre". Voice B measures the average squared distance from c. Voice A measures the average plain distance from c.

Which c makes Voice B's number smallest? Which c makes Voice A's number smallest? Are they the same c?

Test it on a lopsided game first: ₹0 with chance 0.8, ₹10 with chance 0.2.

Hint 1 (after a day)

For the lopsided game, try c = 0 and c = 2 (the mean). Work out both voices' numbers for each. Which c wins for which voice?

Hint 2 (after another day)

For Voice B, write X − c = (X − μ) + (μ − c) and open up the square, as in section 3. For Voice A, move c a tiny step to the right. Which values get closer, and which get further?

The answer (try for three days first)

Voice B is smallest at the mean: the average squared distance from c is Var(X) + (μ − c)², so any c other than μ adds a penalty. Voice A is smallest at the median, the point with half the chance on each side. Moving c right brings the values above it closer and pushes the values below it further away; that only stops helping when the two sides weigh the same. In the lopsided game: from c = 2, Voice B gives 16 and Voice A gives 3.2; from c = 0, Voice B gives 20 and Voice A gives 2. Mean and median are different answers to two different questions.

Variance is the currency of statistics. Here is the first thing it buys: averages of many draws settle down.

Average n rolls of a fair die. The variance of that average is the die's variance, 35/12, divided by n (Unit I, later). Chebyshev then caps the chance that the average lands 0.5 or more away from 3.5. The cap is that variance divided by 0.5².

The cap falls toward 0 as n grows, so the average settles on the mean. That is the first theorem that makes sampling work. Chebyshev proved it this way in 1867.

Its name, and where variance turns up next

That theorem is the weak law of large numbers.

Standard errors, confidence intervals and the efficiency of estimators (Units III–IV) are all statements about variances.

Data science leans on the same idea under the name concentration inequalities. Chebyshev's sharper cousins (Hoeffding, Bernstein) tell you how many samples a model needs.

"Least squares" fitting picks the line that makes a variance-like sum smallest. Your pocket question is its seed.

Why this matters for you

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

(1) Markov: if Y ≥ 0 and a > 0, then P(Y ≥ a) ≤ E[Y]/a. (2) Deduce Chebyshev.

Worked proof

(1) Compare Y with a simpler variable Z: Z = a when Y ≥ a, and Z = 0 otherwise. Whenever Y ≥ a, Z = a ≤ Y; otherwise Z = 0 ≤ Y, because Y is never negative. So Z ≤ Y always, and averages keep that order: E[Z] ≤ E[Y]. But Z takes only two values, so E[Z] = a · P(Y ≥ a). Divide by a > 0:

P(Y ≥ a) ≤ E[Y]/a

The step "otherwise Z = 0 ≤ Y" is exactly where Y ≥ 0 is used, and where the corpus statement goes wrong.

(2) Take Y = (X − μ)², never negative, with E[Y] = σ², and a = k²σ². The events |X − μ| ≥ kσ and (X − μ)² ≥ k²σ² are the same event, so

P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ σ²/(k²σ²) = 1/k² ∎

Equality needs Z = Y almost always: Y is 0 or exactly a. That's the "extreme" shape in the picture: mass 1/(2k²) at each of μ ± kσ, the rest at μ.

An ISI entrance problem

Here is a real ISI question, in plain words first. A random number X always lands between 1 and 10, and its mean is 2.8. Show that its standard deviation can never be more than 3.6.

Source: ISI JRF in Statistics 2026 (STA), Q5(a). In the paper's notation, X takes values in the interval [1, 10] and E[X] = 2.8.

Worked solution

The trick is a product that can't be negative. Since 1 ≤ X ≤ 10, both X − 1 ≥ 0 and 10 − X ≥ 0. So (X − 1)(10 − X) ≥ 0 for every outcome, and its expectation is too.

Expand and use linearity:

E[(X − 1)(10 − X)] = −E[X²] + 11E[X] − 10 ≥ 0

So E[X²] ≤ 11(2.8) − 10 = 20.8. Then

Var(X) = E[X²] − 2.8² ≤ 20.8 − 7.84 = 12.96

and σ ≤ √12.96 = 3.6. ∎

It's the best possible bound. Equality needs (X − 1)(10 − X) = 0, so X sits only at the two ends. Put P(X = 10) = 0.2 and P(X = 1) = 0.8. Then the mean is 0.8 + 2 = 2.8 and E[X²] = 0.8 + 20 = 20.8. So the SD is exactly 3.6.

The same argument gives Var(X) ≤ (b − μ)(μ − a) for any X in [a, b]. That is sharper than the corpus's (b − a)²/4, unless μ is the midpoint.

Checked by script (practice bank, ISI checks folder, file jrf-sta-2026-q5). Across 100,000 random distributions on [1, 10], (10 − μ)(μ − 1) − Var was never negative. The two-point law above gives SD 3.6000. Part (b) of the question needs independence of two variables, which comes in L15.
