Moment Generating Functions — a Barcode for a Distribution

Ten dice, sixty million ways, and a trick that adds them all. ~25 min.

TODAY'S WIN  You'll find an MGF, read the mean and variance off it, name a distribution from it, and find the distribution of a sum by multiplying.

0 · Spaced recall — no peeking

• Each try succeeds with chance 1/3. What is P(first success after the 2nd try)?

• Poisson(λ): what are its mean and variance, and what is P(X = x)?

Answers: (2/3)² = 4/9, the first two tries both miss (L10). And λ, λ, and e−λλx/x! (L9). Both reappear today.

1 · Ten dice

The stall owner rolls two dice. "Total of 7, you win." Easy to price: you list the splits, 1+6, 2+5, 3+4, 4+3, 5+2, 6+1. Six of the 36 pairs, so 6/36.

Then he grins and pulls out ten dice. "Total of exactly 35, you win ₹100. Costs ₹10."

Your puzzle · 4 minutes, pen and paper

Try a trick on two dice first. Write one die as a polynomial, one term per face: (s + s² + s³ + s⁴ + s⁵ + s⁶)/6.

  1. Multiply two copies together. When you multiply s² by s⁵, what power do you get? Why does that match a total of 7?
  2. How many terms in the product come out as s⁷? Compare with the six splits above.
  3. Ten dice can land in 610 ways, over 60 million. Could you list the ones that total 35?

Voice A: "It's the same job as two dice, just longer. Keep listing splits."

Voice B: "Sixty million can't be listed. Multiplying powers adds the exponents, which is exactly how totals add. Multiply instead of listing."

Which voice would you trust with ten dice?

2 · Why a new tool?

You've been computing means and variances one sum at a time: Σ x p(x), then Σ x² p(x), each with its own trick (L8's factorial moments, L10's first step). And one question you can't yet answer at all: if X and Y are independent Poisson counts, what is the distribution of X + Y? Doing it by hand means adding P(X = i)P(Y = s − i) over every split of s, for every s.

One function packs all of this together. The moment generating function (MGF) of X is

M(t) = E[etX],  for every t in some interval around 0 where this average is finite.

It's just L6's E[g(X)] with g(x) = etx, one average for each value of t. Keep the weights, move the values to etx, average. Two facts hold for every MGF: M(0) = E[1] = 1, and its graph curves upward.

3 · What you already know: the series for e

The puzzle's trick works because powers add when you multiply: sa × sb = sa+b. Put s = et and the die polynomial becomes E[etX], the MGF. So the MGF is the puzzle's trick, written for any variable.

Why e? Because of a series you met in L9. For any number u:

eu = 1 + u + u²/2! + u³/3! + …

Put u = tX and take the average of every term. Linearity (L6) lets you average term by term:

M(t) = 1 + E[X] t + E[X²] t²/2! + E[X³] t³/3! + …

Look at that. The averages E[X], E[X²], E[X³] hang on the function like clothes on a washing line, one on each power of t. These averages are called the moments of X. That's the name: a function that generates the moments.

To take one moment off the line, use the slope. The slope of M at t = 0 picks out the coefficient of t, which is E[X]. Slope twice picks out E[X²]. You'll see this as a picture in section 5.

4 · Three MGFs you already own

Binomial. Put etx inside L8's formula and group it with px:

M(t) = Σ C(n, x)(pet)xqn−x = (q + pet)n

The last step is the binomial expansion of (a + b)n, which is the same pattern-counting as L8: each of the n factors contributes either a or b.

Poisson. Group etx with λx and use the series for eu (L9):

M(t) = e−λ Σ (λet)x/x! = eλ(et − 1)

Geometric (trials, L10). A geometric series in qet, finite only while qet < 1:

M(t) = Σ etk qk−1p = pet/(1 − qet),  for t < −ln q.

5 · Reading moments off the curve

Why "moment generating"? Differentiate under the average: M′(t) = E[XetX], so at t = 0, M′(0) = E[X]. Again: M″(0) = E[X²], and the k-th derivative at 0 is E[Xk]. The slope at 0 is the mean. Here are three distributions that all have mean 2. Predict first.

Check it on the binomial: M′(t) = n(q + pet)n−1pet, so M′(0) = n(q + p)n−1p = np, the mean from L8, in one line.

Say it in one line

The MGF stores every moment (E[Xk] = M(k)(0)), names the distribution (if two variables have the same MGF near 0, they have the same distribution: this is the uniqueness theorem), and turns sums into products for independent variables.

A barcode. Scan the barcode and the till knows exactly which product it is: no two products share one. The MGF is a distribution's barcode.

Where it breaks: some distributions have no barcode at all. If E[etX] is infinite for every t > 0 (or every t < 0), there is no MGF. And knowing every moment is not always a barcode: the lognormal distribution shares all its moments with other distributions. It's the MGF's existence on an interval around 0 that makes the uniqueness theorem work.

6 · Sums become products

First a definition we've used informally. Random variables X and Y are independent random variables if every event about X is independent of every event about Y: P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x)P(Y = y) for all x, y (L2's multiplication rule, for every pair). Then averages of products split:

E[g(X)h(Y)] = ΣxΣy g(x)h(y)P(X = x)P(Y = y) = E[g(X)] E[h(Y)]

since the double sum factors into two single sums. Take g = etx and h = ety, and since et(X+Y) = etXetY:

MX+Y(t) = MX(t) MY(t)  for independent X and Y.

That's Voice B's trick from the puzzle, for any independent variables. Voice B wins: multiply ten die polynomials and read off the s35 term. It comes to 4,395,456 ways out of 60,466,176, a chance of about 0.073. Pay ₹10 to win ₹100 at chance 0.073, and over 100 bets you pay ₹1,000 and win about ₹730. The owner wins again.

Watch it settle the Poisson question. Predict before step 3.

"Every distribution has an MGF, and the product rule always works." Both are false. Take P(X = k) = 1/(k(k + 1)) for k = 1, 2, … (these add to 1, since each term is 1/k − 1/(k + 1)). For any t > 0 the terms etk/(k(k + 1)) grow without bound, so E[etX] is infinite: no MGF. (It doesn't even have a mean; compare L6's Cauchy.) And the product rule needs independence: if X is a fair 0/1 coin and Y = X, then X + Y = 2X has MGF (1 + e2t)/2, but MX(t)² = ((1 + et)/2)², which is different. Compare L6: E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] needs no independence; the MGF rule does.

7 · Worked examples: identify, then add

(a) X has MGF M(t) = e3(et − 1). Name its distribution and find P(X = 2) and Var(X). (b) X ~ Bin(5, 0.3) and Y ~ Bin(7, 0.3) are independent. Find the distribution of X + Y. (c) From M(t) = (0.6 + 0.4et)10, find E[X] by differentiating.

(a) This is the Poisson barcode with λ = 3, so by uniqueness X ~ Poisson(3). Then

P(X = 2) = e−3 3²/2! = 4.5 × 0.04979 = 0.2240

and Var(X) = λ = 3.

(b) Multiply:

(0.7 + 0.3et)5(0.7 + 0.3et)7 = (0.7 + 0.3et)12

the Bin(12, 0.3) barcode. So X + Y ~ Bin(12, 0.3), which makes sense: 5 trials then 7 more, all with the same p. (With different p's the product is not a binomial barcode, and the sum isn't binomial.)

(c) M′(t) = 10(0.6 + 0.4et)9 × 0.4et, so M′(0) = 10 × 1 × 0.4 = 4. That's np for Bin(10, 0.4), as the barcode says.

In one or two sentences of your own: why does an MGF turn the hard problem "distribution of X + Y" into the easy problem "multiply two functions"?

Compare with a model answer

Because et(X+Y) = etXetY, and for independent variables the average of a product is the product of the averages. Then uniqueness lets us read the distribution of X + Y back off the product, without adding up every split by hand.

Your turn, half done. X ~ Poisson(1.5) and Y ~ Poisson(2.5) are independent. Find P(X + Y = 0).

Step 1: multiply the MGFs:

e1.5(et−1) e2.5(et−1) = eλ(et−1)

with λ = ____ . Step 2: so X + Y ~ Poisson(____), and P(X + Y = 0) = e−λ = ____ .

Check your steps

λ = 1.5 + 2.5 = 4, so X + Y ~ Poisson(4) and P(X + Y = 0) = e^(−4) ≈ 0.0183. Directly: X + Y = 0 needs both zero, e^(−1.5)e^(−2.5) = e^(−4), the same.

Your pocket question · carry it for a few days

Can you renumber two dice, using positive whole numbers but not the usual 1 to 6, so that their totals have exactly the same chances as two ordinary dice? Every total from 2 to 12, with the same chance as before.

It sounds impossible. Use today's trick. Take one hint at a time.

Hint 1 (after a day)

An ordinary die's polynomial breaks into pieces:

s + s² + … + s⁶ = s(1 + s)(1 + s + s²)(1 − s + s²)

Two dice: that, squared. Can you share the eight pieces out between two new dice in a different way?

Hint 2 (after another day)

Each new die needs six faces, so its polynomial must equal 6 when s = 1. And each needs the piece s, so that no face is 0. The piece (1 − s + s²) equals 1 at s = 1, so it can move freely.

The answer (try for three days first)

Give one die both copies of (1 − s + s²) and the other die none. You get faces 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4 and 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8. Their totals match ordinary dice exactly; check a few. These are the Sicherman dice, found by George Sicherman and made famous by Martin Gardner in 1978. They're the only other way to do it with positive faces. The barcode of the sum is the same, so the distribution of the sum is the same: that's uniqueness at work.

The MGF is where probability starts using calculus as a machine. Its key move: for independent sums, MGFs multiply. That one move proves facts you'll lean on for years.

Where the MGF goes next

The central limit theorem is proved by showing that the MGF of a standardised sum tends to et²/2. That is the normal's barcode (L13), and your corpus does exactly this.

The log of the MGF, the cumulant generating function, adds up over independent sums. That is why variances, the second cumulant, add.

Its cousin E[eitX], the characteristic function, exists for every distribution, Cauchy included. It is the Fourier transform of the density, and it's what measure-theoretic probability uses.

MGFs also prove that a sum of squared standard normals is chi-square (L14). That fact is behind every t-test and F-test.

Large-deviation bounds (Chernoff) are Markov's inequality (L7) applied to etX.

Why this matters for you

EXAM  Expect "name the distribution with MGF …" (match the barcode), and the mean and variance from M′(0) and M″(0). Expect Ma+bX(t) = eatMX(bt), and adding properties: binomials with the same p add, and Poissons add.

True or false: "every distribution has an MGF" is false. "The MGF settles the distribution" is true, when it exists near 0.

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

(1) Show that Ma+bX(t) = eatMX(bt). (2) Show that E[Xk] is k! times the coefficient of tk in the power series of M(t), and use it on the Poisson to get E[X²] = λ² + λ.

Worked proof

(1)

E[et(a+bX)] = E[eate(bt)X] = eatMX(bt)

using linearity to pull out the constant eat. ∎

(2) Expand etX = 1 + tX + t²X²/2! + … and average term by term (allowed when the MGF exists near 0):

M(t) = 1 + E[X]t + E[X²]t²/2! + E[X³]t³/3! + …

So the coefficient of tk is E[Xk]/k!. ∎ For the Poisson, expand eλ(et−1) with et − 1 = t + t²/2 + …:

M(t) = 1 + λ(t + t²/2) + λ²t²/2 + … = 1 + λt + (λ + λ²)t²/2 + …

so E[X] = λ and E[X²] = λ + λ², giving Var(X) = λ once more.

An ISI entrance problem

Here is a real ISI question, in plain words first. Add up n independent copies of the same random number X. You're told the total is binomial, Bin(nk, p). Must each copy then be Bin(k, p)? Say true or false, with reasons.

Source: ISI M.Stat 2020 descriptive paper (PSB), Q4(b). In the paper's notation, X1, …, Xn are independent and identically distributed, Sn = X1 + … + Xn, and Sn ~ Bin(nk, p).

Worked solution

True. Let M be the common MGF of the Xi. They're independent, so the product rule, applied n − 1 times, gives MSn(t) = M(t)n. We're told Sn ~ Bin(nk, p), so

M(t)n = (q + pet)nk

Two checks make the step back safe:

CheckWhy it holds
M exists for every tSn lies between 0 and nk, so each Xi is bounded too
there is exactly one root to takeM(t) is an average of positive numbers etX, so it is positive; a positive number has exactly one positive n-th root

Taking that root:

M(t) = (q + pet)k

That's the Bin(k, p) barcode. So by uniqueness, each Xi ~ Bin(k, p).

Part (a) of the question asks the same about exponential sums, and there the answer is false. The n-th root of an exponential's MGF is the MGF of a gamma distribution, not an exponential (L12 and L14).

Checked by script (practice bank, ISI checks, file psb-2020-q4). Convolving Bin(3, 0.3) with itself four times reproduces Bin(12, 0.3) to within 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁶. A simulation confirms part (a) is false.
