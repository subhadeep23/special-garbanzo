What Probability Really Means

Starting from zero, in plain language. No formulas until we actually need them. ~15 min.

TODAY'S WIN  By the end you'll be able to say, in your own words, what probability is, state the three simple rules every probability must follow, and use them to answer an "at least one" question in two lines, with three new words you'll meet gently along the way.

1 · The plain idea

Probability is just a way of putting a number on how likely something is.

We agree on a scale from 0 to 1:

• 0 means "this never happens."

• 1 means "this always happens, for sure."

• 0.5 means "it's a coin toss — half the time."

That's the whole heart of it. A weather app saying "70% chance of rain" is just saying the probability of rain is 0.7 — closer to the "sure" end than the "never" end. Everything else in this subject is built on top of this one simple idea.

2 · Three words, introduced gently

To talk about likelihood precisely, we need three small words. You already understand all three from daily life — we're just giving them their proper names.

Word 1 — outcome

An outcome is one single thing that could happen. Roll a die: getting a 4 is one outcome. Getting a 6 is another.

Word 2 — sample space

Now imagine a bag holding every outcome that's possible. That whole collection is the sample space. For a die, the sample space is the six faces: {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Nothing outside the bag can happen; everything inside might.

Think of it as the complete "list of everything that could occur." Later you'll see it written with the symbol \(\Omega\) (the Greek letter omega) — but the symbol is just shorthand for "the bag of all outcomes." Don't let the symbol worry you.

Word 3 — event

An event is a question you can answer yes-or-no about the result — usually a group of outcomes you care about. "Did the die show an even number?" is an event. It's happy with three outcomes: 2, 4, or 6. So an event is simply a chosen bunch of outcomes from the bag.

3 · The three rules probability must obey

Whatever numbers we hand out as probabilities, they have to make common sense. It turns out three simple rules are enough to keep everything honest. Here they are in plain words first:

1No negatives. A likelihood can't be less than zero. The smallest allowed is 0 ("never").
2Something must happen. The probability that some outcome in the bag occurs is exactly 1. (The die shows a face — guaranteed.)
3Separate things add up. If two events can't happen together, the chance of "one or the other" is just their chances added. Chance of rolling a 2 or a 5 = chance of 2 + chance of 5.

That's it. These three are called the axioms of probability — "axiom" is just the academic word for a starting rule we agree to, and build everything else on. They were written down in this clean form by a Russian mathematician, Kolmogorov, in 1933. You don't need his machinery today; you just need the three common-sense rules above.

Before you touch the picture: a fair die, and the event "the roll is at least 3". Make a guess. Is its probability nearer 1/3, 1/2 or 2/3? And what is the chance it does not happen?

Check your guess

Four faces (3, 4, 5, 6) out of six, so 4/6 = 2/3. The other two faces hold the rest, 2/6 = 1/3. Now build it below and watch the two parts fill the bar.

Try the three rules with your own hands. Pick some faces to make an event, and watch where its probability lands between 0 and 1.

Say it in one line

A probability is a number given to every event, never negative, with the whole sample space getting exactly 1, and with non-overlapping events adding.

One cake, shared out. Think of probability as a single cake of total size 1, cut among the outcomes. No slice is negative (Rule 1). All the slices together are the whole cake (Rule 2). Two events that share no outcomes are separate plates of slices, so together they hold the sum (Rule 3). An event's probability is how much cake is on its plate.

Where it breaks: a real cake has finitely many slices. A dart thrown at a line has infinitely many possible landing points, and each single point gets a slice of size 0, yet the cake is still whole (Lesson 5). So you can't always find a probability by adding up one slice per outcome.

"It either happens or it doesn't, so it's 50–50." That's the most common wrong idea about probability. Two possible answers to a yes/no question do not make them equally likely. Take "the die shows a 6". It either happens or it doesn't, yet its probability is 1/6, not 1/2: one face says yes and five say no. Equal chances need equally likely outcomes underneath, and you count those, not the two answers.

4 · One tiny result, to see the rules working

Here's the pattern you'll use constantly. Suppose the chance of rain tomorrow is 0.7. What's the chance of no rain?

"Rain" and "no rain" can't both happen, and together they cover everything (Rule 2 says everything totals 1). So:

chance of no rain  =  1 − 0.7  =  0.3

This "1 minus the chance" move has a name — the complement rule — and we didn't have to memorize it. It fell straight out of Rules 2 and 3. That's the whole spirit of this course. The event "not A" is called the complement of A, written Aᶜ, and the rule is:

P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A)

5 · Worked example: "at least one" by the back door

Two fair dice are rolled. What is the chance that at least one of them shows a 4?

Step 1 — the bag. Each die has 6 faces, so there are 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely pairs, like (1, 4) or (4, 4).

Step 2 — turn the question around. "At least one 4" covers many cases: a 4 on the first, on the second, or on both. Its complement is a single clean case: no 4 anywhere. Each die then has 5 allowed faces, so there are 5 × 5 = 25 such pairs.

Step 3 — the complement rule.

P(at least one 4) = 1 − 25/36 = 11/36

Check by counting directly: 6 pairs have a 4 first, 6 have a 4 second, and (4, 4) is in both lists, so it was counted twice. That's 6 + 6 − 1 = 11 pairs. Same answer.

WATCH OUT  The corpus works this same example and writes

P(A₁ ∪ A₂) = P(A₁) + P(A₂) + P(A₁ ∩ A₂)

The last sign must be a minus: the overlap (4, 4) was counted twice, so you take it off once. Its next lines do subtract, and the printed answer, 11/36, is right. Re-derive every sign; an OCR copy can flip one.

In one or two sentences of your own: why does the complement rule follow from Rules 2 and 3? Name the two events and say which rule each step uses.

Compare with a model answer

A and "not A" can't both happen, so by Rule 3, P(A or not A) = P(A) + P(not A). But "A or not A" is the whole bag, so by Rule 2 it equals 1. Hence P(not A) = 1 − P(A).

Your turn, half done. Toss a fair coin three times. What is the chance of at least one head?

Step 1: the bag has 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 equally likely outcomes. Step 2: the complement of "at least one head" is ____, which is just ____ outcome. Step 3: so the answer is 1 − ____ = ____ .

Check your steps

The complement is "no heads at all", which is only TTT: 1 outcome, so 1/8. Then P(at least one head) = 1 − 1/8 = 7/8.

The three rules say how probabilities must behave, not what they mean. Statistics argues about the meaning. A frequentist reads 0.7 as "it rains on about 70% of days like this, in the long run". A Bayesian reads it as a degree of belief that new data will update (that's Lesson 3). Both obey the same three rules, which is why Kolmogorov's axioms became the shared ground of the whole subject. At ISI depth, rule 3 is stated for an infinite sequence of events, and the events form a σ-field. That's measure theory, and you'll meet it gently in Lesson 4.

Why this matters for you

EXAM  UGC-NET 107 asks directly which statements follow from the axioms (for example, P(∅) = 0 or P(A) ≤ 1), and many counting questions are "at least one" questions in disguise, best done by the complement. Almost every result you'll meet later — adding probabilities, "at least one", complements — is squeezed out of these three rules. So if a formula ever slips your mind in the exam, you can often rebuild it from these three sentences: fewer things to memorize, more things you can reconstruct.

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

Using only the three rules, prove that for any two events A and B (which may overlap):

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)

Here ∪ means "or" (at least one happens) and ∩ means "and" (both happen).

Worked proof

Cut A ∪ B into two pieces that don't overlap: all of A, and the part of B outside A, written B − A. By Rule 3,

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B − A)

Now cut B itself into two non-overlapping pieces: the part inside A, A ∩ B, and the part outside, B − A. Rule 3 again gives

P(B − A) = P(B) − P(A ∩ B)

Substitute it into the first line and you're done. ∎

Notice the minus sign comes out of the proof by itself, which is exactly the sign the corpus slip lost. The same trick (cut into non-overlapping pieces, then add) proves P(∅) = 0 and P(A) ≤ 1 too.

An ISI entrance problem

From the ISI JRF Statistics 2020 paper (STA), Q4(a): "Suppose that there are five pairs of shoes in a closet and four shoes are taken out at random. What is the probability that, among the four which are taken out, there is at least one complete pair?"

Worked solution

"At least one pair" is messy (one pair, or two pairs). Its complement is clean: no complete pair, so the four shoes come from four different pairs.

Counting the bag. Pick the shoes one at a time: 10 choices, then 9, then 8, then 7, so 10 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 5040 ordered picks. But the same four shoes can be picked in 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24 different orders, and we only care which four we hold. So there are 5040 ÷ 24 = 210 different handfuls, all equally likely. (This "choose 4 from 10" count is written C(10, 4); you'll use it constantly later, and this is all it means.)

Counting no pair. Leave out one of the 5 pairs entirely: 5 ways. From each of the other 4 pairs take the left or the right shoe: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 ways. That's 5 × 16 = 80.

P(at least one pair) = 1 − 80/210 = 13/21

Checked by listing all 210 four-shoe draws by computer: 80 have no pair, so the answer 13/21 ≈ 0.619 is exact.
