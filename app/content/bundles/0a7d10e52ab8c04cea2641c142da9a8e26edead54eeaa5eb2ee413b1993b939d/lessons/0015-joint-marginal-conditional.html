Joint, Marginal and Conditional — Two Variables at Once

Totals, slices, and the test for "no link". ~22 min.

TODAY'S WIN  From a joint table or a joint density you'll be able to get each variable's own distribution (add or integrate out the other), get a conditional distribution (slice and rescale), and decide whether the two are independent, including the quick test that settles most exam questions in one glance.

0 · Spaced recall — no peeking

• P(A) = 0.01, P(+ | A) = 0.9, P(+ | not A) = 0.1. Roughly what is P(A | +)?

• When are random variables X and Y independent (L11)?

Answers: 0.009/(0.009 + 0.099) ≈ 0.08 (L3: the rare-condition trap). And when every event about X is independent of every event about Y; for discrete variables, P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x)P(Y = y) for all x, y.

1 · The whole table

Twenty students are sorted two ways at once: by how much they studied (X = 1, 2, 3, low to high) and by their grade band (Y = 1, 2, 3). Each cell counts the students with that pair of values. Divide by 20 and the table is the joint distribution of X and Y: the chance of every pair, p(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y). All cells are non-negative and add to 1, the same two checks as L5, now on a grid.

Everything about the pair is in that table. Two kinds of question come up again and again:

1About one variable alone. "What share of students got grade band 3?" Add down the column, whatever X was. Each variable's own distribution, read off the table's totals, is its marginal distribution: P(X = x) = Σy p(x, y), the row totals, sitting in the table's margin, hence the name.
2About one variable, given news about the other. "Among students who studied most, how are grades spread?" Keep only that row and rescale it to add to 1. That's the conditional distribution, P(Y = y | X = x) = p(x, y)/P(X = x): L2's "news shrinks the bag", with a whole row as the bag.

Pick rows in two tables and predict first.

Say it in one line

Marginal = add the other variable out; conditional = slice and rescale. X and Y are independent exactly when every slice rescales to the same shape, which is the same as the joint table being the product of its margins: p(x, y) = pX(x) pY(y) for every cell.

A spreadsheet with totals. The joint table is the full sheet; the marginals are its totals row and totals column; a conditional is one row filtered out and re-expressed as percentages of its own total. Independence means every filtered row shows the same percentages as the totals row.

Where it breaks: for continuous variables there are no rows to filter: any exact slice X = x0 has probability 0 (L5). The conditional is then defined as a ratio of densities, f(y | x) = f(x, y)/fX(x), not by "filtering a row with some people in it". It works the same way in practice, but it is a density per unit of y, not a percentage.

2 · Densities: integrate out, slice, rescale

For continuous X and Y, a joint density f(x, y) spreads probability over the plane, and volumes under it are probabilities. Sums become integrals:

fX(x) = ∫ f(x, y) dy,    f(y | x) = f(x, y)/fX(x)

Take f(x, y) = x + y on the unit square. Slide the slice and predict first.

"The support is a rectangle, so X and Y are independent." Not enough. f(x, y) = x + y lives on the unit square, a perfect rectangle, yet the slices in the picture change with x₀, so X and Y are dependent. Independence needs the density itself to factorise: f(x, y) = g(x)h(y). Here the marginals multiply to (x + 1/2)(y + 1/2), which is not x + y. The reverse test is decisive: if the support is not a rectangle (a triangle like 0 < x < y < 1, a disc, a parallelogram), X and Y cannot be independent, because learning X narrows the range Y can take. The ISI problem below is exactly this.

3 · Worked example: the density x + y

Let f(x, y) = x + y for 0 < x, y < 1. (a) Check it's a joint density. (b) Find fX and f(y | x). (c) Find P(X + Y ≤ 1). (d) Are X and Y independent?

(a) It's non-negative, and the total volume is

∫∫(x + y) = ∫∫ x + ∫∫ y = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1

(b) Integrate y out: fX(x) = ∫01(x + y) dy = x + 1/2. Slice and rescale:

f(y | x) = (x + y)/(x + 1/2),  0 < y < 1

(c) The region below the line x + y = 1 is a triangle. Integrate y first, from 0 to 1 − x:

∫01[x(1 − x) + (1 − x)²/2] dx = ∫01(1 − x²)/2 dx = 1/3

Sanity check: the density is lowest near (0, 0), so the lower-left half holds less than half the probability.

(d) By symmetry fY(y) = y + 1/2, and (x + 1/2)(y + 1/2) ≠ x + y (try x = y = 0: 1/4 against 0). Not independent.

In one or two sentences of your own: why does a non-rectangular support rule out independence immediately?

Compare with a model answer

If the support isn't a rectangle, some x-value and some y-value are each possible on their own but impossible together. Then P(X near x, Y near y) = 0 while P(X near x)P(Y near y) > 0, so the joint can't be the product of the marginals.

Your turn, half done. With the same density x + y, find P(X < 1/2).

Step 1: only X matters, so use the marginal fX(x) = x + 1/2. Step 2: integrate it from 0 to 1/2:

[x²/2 + x/2]01/2 = ____ + ____ = ____ .

Check your steps

1/8 + 1/4 = 3/8. Less than 1/2, because the density leans toward larger x.

Joint distributions are where statistics proper begins, because real questions are about relationships. Regression is the study of the conditional mean E[Y | X = x] as x varies (the slice means in today's pictures); a straight-line regression is the special case where those means fall on a line, which is exactly what happens for the bivariate normal (Unit I, later). Covariance and correlation summarise the joint table in one number each. Contingency-table χ² tests (Unit V) ask whether an observed two-way table could come from independent margins: today's Table A versus Table B, with real data. And Bayes' theorem (L3) is just the joint table read in both directions: p(x, y) = p(x)p(y | x), and also p(y)p(x | y).

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

Prove the factorisation test: if f(x, y) = g(x)h(y) on a rectangle a < x < b, c < y < d (and 0 elsewhere), for some non-negative functions g and h, then X and Y are independent, and their marginals are g and h rescaled.

Worked proof

Let G = ∫ab g and H = ∫cd h. The total volume is 1, so GH = 1. Integrate out y:

fX(x) = g(x) ∫cd h(y) dy = H g(x)

and likewise fY(y) = G h(y). Then

fX(x)fY(y) = GH g(x)h(y) = f(x, y)

everywhere, so the joint density is the product of the marginals: X and Y are independent. ∎ The rectangle matters: on a triangle, "f = g(x)h(y) inside and 0 outside" is not a product, because the outside zero depends on x and y together.

An ISI entrance problem

From the ISI M.Stat 2024 objective paper (PSA), Q21: (X, Y) is a point chosen uniformly from the parallelogram with vertices (0, 1), (2, 1), (0, −1) and (−2, −1). Which are true? (i) X's marginal is uniform on (−2, 2). (ii) E(XY) = 0. (iii) X and Y are independent. Options: (A) only (ii) and (iii); (B) only (ii); (C) only (i); (D) none.

Worked solution

Describe the region. At height y (between −1 and 1) the parallelogram runs from x = y − 1 to x = y + 1, a width of 2. The area is 4, so the density is 1/4 on the region.

(iii) is false at a glance: the support is not a rectangle. For example X = 1.5 forces Y > 0.5.

(i) is false. Integrate y out: for a given x, y runs over the part of (−1, 1) within 1 of x, which has length 2 − |x|. So fX(x) = (2 − |x|)/4 on (−2, 2): a triangle, not flat.

(ii) is false. Each horizontal slice has the same width 2, so Y ~ U(−1, 1), and given Y the offset U = X − Y is uniform on (−1, 1) whatever Y is: U is independent of Y. Then, by linearity and L11's product rule,

E(XY) = E(Y²) + E(U)E(Y) = 1/3 + 0 = 1/3

None is true: option (D).

Checked by script (the practice bank's ISI checks, file psa-2024-q21, written for this lesson): the marginal of X matches (2 − |x|)/4 on a grid of 39 points; two million simulated points give E(XY) = 0.3327 (standard error 0.0003) against 1/3; and P(X > 1, Y < 0) = 0 while P(X > 1)P(Y < 0) ≈ 0.06.
