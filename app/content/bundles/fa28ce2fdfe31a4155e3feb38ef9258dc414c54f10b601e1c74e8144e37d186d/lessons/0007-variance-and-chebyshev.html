Variance and Chebyshev — How Spread Out?

The balance point says where. Variance says how wide. ~20 min.

TODAY'S WIN  You'll be able to compute a variance with Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])², handle Var(aX + b) in one line, and use Chebyshev's inequality to bound how much probability can lie far from the mean when you know nothing about the shape.

• P(X = x) = x/10 for x = 1, 2, 3, 4. What were E[X] and E[X²]?

• Discrete X has F(5) = 0.9 and F(5−) = 0.6. What is P(X = 5)?

Answers: 3 and 10, and 10 is bigger than 3² = 9 (L6). And 0.3, the jump at 5 (L4–L5). That gap of 1 between 10 and 9 is today's main character.

1 · Same balance point, different spread

Three street games, each worth 3 rupees a play on average:

AYou always get exactly 3.
BA coin decides: 1 rupee or 5 rupees.
CYou get 1, 2, 3 or 4 with chances 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 (L6's weights).

All three balance at 3, yet they feel completely different. A never surprises you; B always lands 2 away from its mean. We need a second number: how far, typically, does X land from its balance point?

The natural idea is "the average distance from the mean". Statistics uses the average squared distance instead, because squares behave well in algebra (you'll see why in a minute). The variance of X is

Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²],  where μ = E[X].

It's a weighted average, exactly like L6: each squared distance (x − μ)² counts with its probability. Game A: every distance is 0, so the variance is 0. Game B: every distance is 2, so the variance is 2² = 4.

Squaring changed the units: rupees became rupees². To get back to rupees, take the square root. That's the standard deviation, σ = √Var(X), a typical distance from the mean in the units of X. Game B's is 2.

2 · The shortcut you'll use every time

Expand the square and use linearity from L6 (μ is just a number):

E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − 2μE[X] + μ² = E[X²] − μ²

Say it in one line

Variance = mean of the square minus square of the mean: Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])². It is the gap you met in L6, and it is never negative, because it's an average of squares.

Game C: E[X²] = 10 and E[X] = 3, so Var(X) = 10 − 9 = 1 and σ = 1. The mystery gap from last lesson was the variance all along.

"Mean of the square, minus square of the mean: never below zero, the gap in between." The order is the one thing people swap. Get it backwards and you'll compute 9 − 10 = −1, an impossible negative variance. The rhyme also tells you the sanity check.

3 · Shift it, stretch it

What happens to the spread if every value is shifted by b, or multiplied by a? Predict before step 3.

Var(aX + b) = a² Var(X)  and  SD(aX + b) = |a| SD(X)

The b drops out because sliding doesn't change distances. The a comes out squared because variance measures squared distances, which is also why a negative a still gives a positive variance.

Spinning the beam. Put L6's beam on an axle through its balance point and spin it. How hard it is to spin (its moment of inertia) is the sum of each weight times its squared distance from the axle: Σ p(x)(x − μ)². That's exactly the variance. Weights far out make the beam hard to spin, just as far-out values make the variance big, and doubling every distance makes it four times harder.

Where it breaks: physics allows any total mass, while probability weights always total 1. And a real beam is finite, but a distribution can stretch so far that its variance is infinite even when its mean exists, so the "spin" number may not exist at all.

4 · Chebyshev: a promise that needs no shape

Often you know the mean and the SD but not the shape of the distribution. Can you still say anything about the chance of landing far from the mean? Yes, and it's surprisingly strong. Chebyshev's inequality: for any X with mean μ and SD σ, and any k > 0,

P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k²  or equivalently  P(|X − μ| < kσ) ≥ 1 − 1/k²

At most a quarter of the probability lies 2 SDs or more away; at most a ninth lies 3 or more away. Any shape. Try to break it.

Why must it be true? Every value at least kσ away contributes at least (kσ)² to the variance's weighted sum, so if a share q of probability sits out there, the variance is at least q k²σ². But the variance is σ², so q ≤ 1/k². Too much mass far away would make the variance too big. (The Go-deeper block writes this out properly.)

"Chebyshev says about 25% of the data lies beyond 2 SDs." No. It says at most 25%, for the worst possible shape. It is a ceiling, not an estimate. In the picture, the bell has only 1/8 beyond 2 SDs and twin peaks has none; only the strange "extreme" shape reaches the ceiling. For the normal curve you'll meet in L13 the true figure is under 5%. Use Chebyshev when the shape is unknown and you need a guarantee; never quote it as the likely value. And it needs no normality, no symmetry, no large sample: just a finite mean and variance.

WATCH OUT  Your corpus proves Chebyshev from Markov's inequality, P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X]/a, and states Markov "for any non-zero quantity a" with no other condition. That's wrong: Markov needs X ≥ 0 and a > 0. Counterexample: X = −10 or 1, each with chance 1/2. Then E[X] = −4.5, but P(X ≥ 1) = 1/2, which is not below −4.5/1. The Chebyshev proof is safe, because it applies Markov to (X − μ)², which is never negative. That's the fifth confirmed slip in this corpus (the scanned page says the same, so it's not an OCR error).

5 · Worked examples: the bound in action

(a) For L6's X (P(X = x) = x/10 on 1, 2, 3, 4), find the variance two ways, then compare Chebyshev's bound for P(|X − 3| ≥ 2) with the truth. (b) A variable has mean 12 and variance 9. Give a lower bound for P(6 < X < 18).

(a) Variance, the shortcut: Var(X) = 10 − 3² = 1, so σ = 1.

The long way, as a check: distances from 3 are −2, −1, 0, 1, so

Var(X) = 4(0.1) + 1(0.2) + 0(0.3) + 1(0.4) = 1

Chebyshev: 2 = 2σ, so k = 2 and P(|X − 3| ≥ 2) ≤ 1/2² = 1/4.

Truth: |x − 3| ≥ 2 happens only at x = 1 (x = 5 isn't possible), so the true probability is P(X = 1) = 1/10. The bound holds, loosely: 1/10 against 1/4.

(b) Here μ = 12 and σ = 3. The event 6 < X < 18 is |X − 12| < 6, and 6 = 2σ, so k = 2:

P(6 < X < 18) ≥ 1 − 1/2² = 1 − 9/36 = 3/4

(The second form, 1 − σ²/c² with c = 6, is how many exam keys write it.) Notice what was not given: the shape. That's exactly when Chebyshev is the right tool.

In one or two sentences of your own: why is Chebyshev's bound usually far above the true tail probability, and when is it exactly right?

Compare with a model answer

The bound has to hold for every possible shape with that mean and SD, so it is set by the worst case, a distribution that puts all its far-away mass exactly at μ ± kσ and the rest at μ. Ordinary shapes spread their mass more evenly and sit well under it; only that extreme shape reaches it.

Your turn, half done. Exam scores have mean 50 and SD 5, shape unknown. Find the narrowest interval centred on 50 that Chebyshev guarantees holds at least 96% of scores.

Step 1: we need 1 − 1/k² = 0.96, so 1/k² = ____ and k = ____ . Step 2: the interval is 50 ± k × 5, which is ____ to ____ .

Check your steps

1/k² = 0.04, so k² = 25 and k = 5. The interval is 50 ± 25, from 25 to 75. A guarantee for any shape is expensive: it needs a band 5 SDs wide.

Variance is the currency of statistics. Here is the first thing it buys: averages of many draws settle down.

Average n rolls of a fair die. The variance of that average is the die's variance, 35/12, divided by n (Unit I, later). Chebyshev then caps the chance that the average lands 0.5 or more away from 3.5. The cap is that variance divided by 0.5².

Its name, and where variance turns up next

That theorem is the weak law of large numbers.

Standard errors, confidence intervals and the efficiency of estimators (Units III–IV) are all statements about variances.

Data science leans on the same idea under the name concentration inequalities. Chebyshev's sharper cousins (Hoeffding, Bernstein) tell you how many samples a model needs.

"Least squares" fitting picks the line that makes a variance-like sum smallest. Your corpus shows the seed of it: E[(X − a)²] is smallest when a is the mean.

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

(1) Markov: if Y ≥ 0 and a > 0, then P(Y ≥ a) ≤ E[Y]/a. (2) Deduce Chebyshev.

Worked proof

(1) Compare Y with a simpler variable Z: Z = a when Y ≥ a, and Z = 0 otherwise. Whenever Y ≥ a, Z = a ≤ Y; otherwise Z = 0 ≤ Y, because Y is never negative. So Z ≤ Y always, and averages keep that order: E[Z] ≤ E[Y]. But Z takes only two values, so E[Z] = a · P(Y ≥ a). Divide by a > 0:

P(Y ≥ a) ≤ E[Y]/a

The step "otherwise Z = 0 ≤ Y" is exactly where Y ≥ 0 is used, and where the corpus statement goes wrong.

(2) Take Y = (X − μ)², never negative, with E[Y] = σ², and a = k²σ². The events |X − μ| ≥ kσ and (X − μ)² ≥ k²σ² are the same event, so

P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ σ²/(k²σ²) = 1/k² ∎

Equality needs Z = Y almost always: Y is 0 or exactly a. That's the "extreme" shape in the picture: mass 1/(2k²) at each of μ ± kσ, the rest at μ.

An ISI entrance problem

Here is a real ISI question, in plain words first. A random number X always lands between 1 and 10, and its mean is 2.8. Show that its standard deviation can never be more than 3.6.

Source: ISI JRF in Statistics 2026 (STA), Q5(a). In the paper's notation, X takes values in the interval [1, 10] and E[X] = 2.8.

Worked solution

The trick is a product that can't be negative. Since 1 ≤ X ≤ 10, both X − 1 ≥ 0 and 10 − X ≥ 0. So (X − 1)(10 − X) ≥ 0 for every outcome, and its expectation is too.

Expand and use linearity:

E[(X − 1)(10 − X)] = −E[X²] + 11E[X] − 10 ≥ 0

So E[X²] ≤ 11(2.8) − 10 = 20.8. Then

Var(X) = E[X²] − 2.8² ≤ 20.8 − 7.84 = 12.96

and σ ≤ √12.96 = 3.6. ∎

It's the best possible bound. Equality needs (X − 1)(10 − X) = 0, so X sits only at the two ends. Put P(X = 10) = 0.2 and P(X = 1) = 0.8. Then the mean is 0.8 + 2 = 2.8 and E[X²] = 0.8 + 20 = 20.8. So the SD is exactly 3.6.

The same argument gives Var(X) ≤ (b − μ)(μ − a) for any X in [a, b]. That is sharper than the corpus's (b − a)²/4, unless μ is the midpoint.

Checked by script (practice bank, ISI checks folder, file jrf-sta-2026-q5). Across 100,000 random distributions on [1, 10], (10 − μ)(μ − 1) − Var was never negative. The two-point law above gives SD 3.6000. Part (b) of the question needs independence of two variables, which comes in L15.
