Expectation — the Balance Point

You can never roll a 3.5. So why do we "expect" it? ~20 min.

TODAY'S WIN  You'll find the mean of any p.m.f. or density, see it as a balance point, and spot why the average of squares beats the square of the average.

0 · Spaced recall — no peeking

• X is uniform on (0, 1/3). What is its density, and is a density that size a problem?

• Three fair coins, X = number of heads. Write down its mass function.

Answers: the density is 3 on that stretch, and that's fine: only areas are probabilities (L5). And p(0), p(1), p(2), p(3) = 1/8, 3/8, 3/8, 1/8, the jump sizes of L4's staircase. Keep that list; you'll use it in a minute.

1 · The stall owner is back

Remember the dart stall from L5? The owner has a new game. After last time, you trust him a little less.

"Roll one fair die. I pay you the face in rupees: ₹1 for a one, ₹6 for a six. Each roll costs ₹4."

Is that a fair price? Will you play 100 times?

Your puzzle · 3 minutes, pen and paper

Work up to it in small steps:

  1. Say you roll 6 times and each face turns up once. How much do you win? How much do you pay?
  2. Now 600 rolls. About how often does each face turn up? What do you win in total?
  3. So what is one roll worth, on average?

You probably got ₹3.5 a roll. Now two voices argue about that number.

Voice A: "So I can expect ₹3.5 a roll. The game is worth ₹3.5, and ₹4 is too much."

Voice B: "Nonsense. No roll ever pays ₹3.5. You can't expect something that can never happen."

Who is right? Write one sentence before you read on.

2 · What you already know: the weighted average

You've done this sum many times, maybe without a name for it. Think of your marks at university.

You got 9 in a big 4-credit course and 6 in a small 1-credit course. Your average is not (9 + 6)/2 = 7.5. The big course counts four times as much:

(9 × 4 + 6 × 1)/5 = 42/5 = 8.4

That's a weighted average. Each number counts in proportion to its weight. Divide the credits by the total, 5, and the weights become 4/5 and 1/5. They add to 1, so no division is needed at the end: 9 × 4/5 + 6 × 1/5 = 8.4.

Now the die. In 600 rolls each face gets about a sixth of the rolls. So each face is a "course" with weight 1/6:

E[X] = (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6)/6 = 3.5

That's the whole idea. The expectation of a discrete X is the weighted average of its values, with the probabilities as weights. It's also called the expected value or mean, written E[X] or μ:

E[X] = Σ x · p(x)  over every value x that X can take.

For your three coins, the weights are the p.m.f. from the recall box:

0(1/8) + 1(3/8) + 2(3/8) + 3(1/8) = 12/8 = 1.5 heads

3 · Who was right? The balance point

Both voices had a point, and a picture settles it. Put each probability on a beam as a weight, at its value. Where must the support go so the beam stays level? Guess for the x/10 weights before you tap them. Then slide the support yourself.

Try the "Both ends" weights: half at 1, half at 4. The beam is level at 2.5, where no weight sits at all. That's the die's 3.5, too.

So Voice B is right that you'll never roll 3.5. Voice A is right about the price. The mean is where the distribution balances, a summary of all the rolls together. It is not a guess about the next roll. Over many rolls you get about ₹3.5 each, and you pay ₹4. You lose about 50 paise a roll. The owner wins again.

The mean is a see-saw's balance point. Each value is a child on the see-saw. A more likely value is a heavier child. Each child pulls with weight × distance from the support.

On the see-sawIn the distribution
where a child sitsa value x
how heavy the child isits chance p(x)
pull = weight × distance(x − t) · p(x)
the level pointthe mean E[X]

Why is the level point always Σ x p(x)? Add up all the pulls around a support at t:

Σ (x − t) p(x) = Σ x p(x) − t Σ p(x) = E[X] − t

The weights add to 1, so the total pull is zero exactly when t = E[X].

Where it breaks: a real see-saw always balances somewhere. A distribution with weights reaching out for ever, thinning too slowly, can pull so hard from far away that there is no balance point. The density 1/(π(1 + x²)) is like that: it has no mean. That's why the definition asks for Σ |x| p(x) to come to an actual number, not an endless one. Your pocket question below plays with this.

Say it in one line

The mean is the balance point: the weighted average E[X] = Σ x p(x), the one place where the pulls on both sides cancel.

4 · Sand on the beam: densities

For a continuous X, the weight isn't in lumps. It's spread like sand, with density f(x) per unit length, as in L5.

Cut the beam into thin strips. A strip at x, dx wide, holds about f(x) · dx of the weight. That's L5's "speed × time = distance". Each strip adds x · f(x) dx to the weighted average. Adding up thin strips is exactly what the long S means:

E[X] = ∫ x f(x) dx  over the whole line

Often you don't even need to work it out. Flat sand on (0, θ) balances in the middle, at θ/2. L5's tent, the same on both sides of 3, balances at 3. When the sand is lopsided, the balance shifts toward the heavy side, and the long S tells you how far.

5 · Change the values, keep the weights

Often you need the average of something worked out from X: its square, a cost, a reward. If X lands on x with chance p(x), then g(X) lands on g(x) with the same chance. The weights stay; only their places on the beam move:

E[g(X)] = Σ g(x) p(x)  or  ∫ g(x) f(x) dx

Watch what that does to the balance point, and predict before the last step.

Straight-line changes are the easy case, and you've seen them in marks. Double everyone's marks, and the class average doubles. Add 5 grace marks to everyone, and the average goes up by 5. For any numbers a and b:

E[aX + b] = a E[X] + b

Its big brother says E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] for any two variables, independent or not. Together these are called linearity of expectation.

"The average of the squares is the square of the average." Wrong, and it's the most common slip with means.

Picture a square plot of land. A coin decides its side: 1 metre or 3 metres. The average side is 2 m. So is the average area 2 × 2 = 4 m²? No. The areas are 1 and 9, so the average area is (1 + 9)/2 = 5 m².

The big plot's area grew much more than the small one's shrank. Squaring stretches big values more, so E[X²] beats (E[X])². The gap between them is never negative. Lesson 7 names it: the variance. The same slip in another costume: E[1/X] is not 1/E[X].

6 · Worked example: weights x/10

X takes the values 1, 2, 3, 4 with P(X = x) = x/10. (a) Check this is a mass function. (b) Find E[X]. (c) Find E[X²] and compare it with (E[X])². (d) Find E[3X + 2].

(a) Two checks. Every value is positive, and (1 + 2 + 3 + 4)/10 = 10/10 = 1. Both checks from L5 pass.

(b) Weighted average. Multiply each value by its weight and add. Each term is x · x/10:

E[X] = (1 + 4 + 9 + 16)/10 = 30/10 = 3

Does 3 make sense? The middle is 2.5, and the heavier weights sit on the right, so the balance moves right. That's the beam you balanced.

(c) Keep the weights, square the values. Each term is x² · x/10:

E[X²] = (1 + 8 + 27 + 64)/10 = 100/10 = 10

And (E[X])² = 3² = 9. Bigger by 1, just like the land plot.

(d) Linearity. E[3X + 2] = 3 × 3 + 2 = 11. If you don't trust it, do it the long way, with values 5, 8, 11, 14:

(5 + 16 + 33 + 56)/10 = 110/10 = 11

A sand version. Let f(x) = x/2 on (0, 2). It's a density: never negative, and its graph is a triangle of base 2 and height 1, so area 1. To find the area under x · x/2 = x²/2, use the school power rule: xn has area function xn+1/(n + 1).

E[X] = ∫02 x · x/2 dx = [x³/6]02 = 8/6 = 4/3

Right of the middle, 1, because the sand piles up toward 2.

In one or two sentences of your own: why does adding grace marks move the average by exactly that much, while squaring pushes the average past the square of the old average?

Compare with a model answer

A straight-line change moves and stretches every value the same way, so the pulls still cancel at the moved point. Squaring stretches big values much more than small ones, so the right side gains extra pull and the balance moves further right.

Your turn, half done. For the same density f(x) = x/2 on (0, 2), find E[X²] and compare it with (E[X])² = 16/9.

Step 1: keep the weights, square the values: E[X²] = ∫02 x² · x/2 dx. Step 2: the thing to find the area under is x³/2; by the power rule its area function is ____ . Step 3: work it out from 0 to 2 to get ____, and check it is bigger than 16/9.

Check your steps

The area function is x⁴/8. From 0 to 2: 16/8 = 2. And 2 > 16/9 ≈ 1.78, as it must be: the average of the squares beats the square of the average.

Your pocket question · carry it for a few days

The stall owner has one last game. He tosses a coin until it lands heads. Heads on the first toss pays ₹2. On the second toss, ₹4. On the third, ₹8. The prize doubles with every toss.

Work out the game's mean. Then ask yourself: how much would you actually pay to play once? Most people say ₹20 at most. Who is wrong, the maths or the people?

Don't look it up; it has a famous name. Think about it on the bus. Take one hint at a time.

Hint 1 (after a day)

Heads first on toss k has chance 1/2k and pays 2k. What does each term of the weighted average come to? How many terms are there?

Hint 2 (after another day)

The mean is 1 + 1 + 1 + … without end. Now say the owner has only ₹1 crore to pay out. Any prize above that, he pays ₹1 crore. What is the mean now?

The answer (try for three days first)

The maths is right: the mean has no end. This is the St Petersburg paradox, from 1713. But a mean is a long-run average, and this long run needs more plays than anyone can live through. With a real ₹1 crore cap, the first 23 tosses add ₹1 each, and the rest add about ₹1.19, so the game is worth about ₹24. People were right. In 1738 Daniel Bernoulli added a second answer: an extra ₹1 means less to you when you already have a lot.

Expectation is the hinge between probability and data. Average many rounds of the die game and you land near 3.5 a roll, its expectation.

Even a chance is an expectation. Score 1 when an event happens and 0 when it doesn't. Take the event "roll a six":

RollScoreChance
1, 2, 3, 4 or 505/6
611/6

The expected score is 0 × 5/6 + 1 × 1/6 = 1/6. That is exactly P(six).

Four places expectation runs the show

The 0-or-1 score is called the indicator of the event, and E[indicator] = P(A) for every event A.

Averages of data. The law of large numbers (Unit I, later) says the average of many independent draws settles on E[X]. Your corpus opens the topic with exactly that remark.

Estimation. An estimator is "unbiased" when E[estimator] = true value. In machine learning, a model is trained to make an expected loss small.

Curved functions. "The average of a function isn't the function of the average" grows into Jensen's inequality: for any convex g, E[g(X)] ≥ g(E[X]).

Measure theory. It writes all of this as one integral, ∫ X dP. That is how your corpus states its theorems on expectation.

Why this matters for you

EXAM  Expect "find c, then find the mean" for a p.m.f. or density. Expect E[aX + b] from E[X], and E[X²] as a step toward the variance (next lesson).

Expect true-or-false lines too. "E[X] always exists": false, the density 1/(π(1 + x²)) has none. "E[1/X] = 1/E[X]": false. "E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] needs independence": false. Nearly every named distribution you meet next comes with its mean.

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

Let X take only the values 0, 1, 2, 3, … Show that

E[X] = P(X ≥ 1) + P(X ≥ 2) + P(X ≥ 3) + …

It's called the tail-sum formula, and it often turns a hard sum into an easy one.

Worked proof

Write p(k) = P(X = k). The term k · p(k) is p(k) added k times. Stack those copies in a table: row 1 holds one copy of every p(k) with k ≥ 1, row 2 one copy of every p(k) with k ≥ 2, and so on. Then p(k) appears in rows 1, 2, …, k: exactly k times, as needed.

Adding row by row instead of column by column, row j is p(j) + p(j + 1) + … = P(X ≥ j). Every entry is non-negative, so the order of adding doesn't change the total. Hence

E[X] = Σk k p(k) = Σj ≥ 1 P(X ≥ j) ∎

Check on a die: P(X ≥ 1), …, P(X ≥ 6) are 6/6, 5/6, …, 1/6, which add to 21/6 = 3.5. ✓

Your corpus states it as Σk ≥ 0 P(X > k), the same sum with the index shifted by one.

An ISI entrance problem

Here is a real ISI question, in plain words first. You've forgotten the code of a 4-digit lock. The true code is equally likely to be any of the 10,000 codes. On average, how many tries do you need with each of these three ways of guessing?

WayHow you guess
(i)try 0000, 0001, … up to 9999, in order
(ii)pick each try at random from all codes; repeats can happen
(iii)pick each try at random, but never repeat a code

Source: ISI M.Stat 2017 descriptive paper (PSB), Q6. In the paper's words, (ii) picks with replacement and (iii) picks without replacement.

Worked solution

Write N = 10,000, and let T be the number of attempts.

(i) The true code's place in the list 0000, 0001, … is equally likely to be 1, 2, …, N. So E[T] = (1 + 2 + … + N)/N.

To add 1 up to N, pair the first and last terms, then the second and second-last, and so on. Each pair sums to N + 1, and there are N/2 pairs. So the sum is N(N + 1)/2, and

E[T] = (N + 1)/2 = 5000.5

(ii) Each attempt hits with chance 1/N, independently of the others. "At least k attempts are needed" means the first k − 1 all missed. So P(T ≥ k) = (1 − 1/N)k−1, by the multiplication rule for independent events (L2). Now use the tail-sum formula you just proved, with r = 1 − 1/N:

E[T] = 1 + r + r² + … = 1/(1 − r) = N = 10,000

Why is 1 + r + r² + … equal to 1/(1 − r) when 0 ≤ r < 1? Call the sum S. Then S − rS = 1.

(iii) Never repeating, until every code is used, just puts all N codes in a random order. Every order is equally likely. So the true code is equally likely to sit in any of the N places.

Why? Swap place i with place j. That turns the orders with the code at i, one for one, into the orders with it at j. So each place gets the same share. T is again equally likely to be 1, …, N, and E[T] = 5000.5, exactly as in (i).

WayAverage number of tries
(i) in order5000.5
(ii) at random, repeats allowed10,000
(iii) at random, no repeats5000.5

The lesson in it: forgetting what you've tried costs you nearly double.

Checked by script (practice bank, ISI checks folder, file psb-2017-q6). 200,000 simulated locks gave means 4993.8, 10000.9 and 4995.9. Each is within 4 standard errors of 5000.5, 10,000 and 5000.5. An exact enumeration for N = 5 gave 3 = (N + 1)/2.
