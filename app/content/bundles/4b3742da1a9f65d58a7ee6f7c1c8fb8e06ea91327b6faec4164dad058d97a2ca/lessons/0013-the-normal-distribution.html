The Normal Distribution — One Bell, Shifted and Stretched

Every normal question becomes one table lookup. ~22 min.

TODAY'S WIN  You'll be able to standardise any normal with Z = (X − μ)/σ and read probabilities from the Φ table, use the 68–95–99.7 rule, and handle sums and differences of independent normals, where the variances always add.

0 · Spaced recall — no peeking

• Chebyshev: at least what share of any distribution lies within 2 SDs of its mean?

• Independent X ~ Poisson(2) and Y ~ Poisson(3): what is X + Y, and how did you know?

Answers: at least 1 − 1/4 = 3/4 (L7). And Poisson(5): the MGFs multiply to the Poisson(5) barcode (L11). Today you'll see how much better than 3/4 a normal does, and the same MGF trick for normals.

1 · The bell

Measure the heights of a thousand adults, or the marks of a thousand candidates, and draw the histogram. Most values crowd near the middle, they thin out symmetrically on both sides, and very extreme values are rare. Smooth that histogram and you get a bell. The normal distribution N(μ, σ²) is the density

f(x) = (1/(σ√(2π))) e−(x−μ)²/(2σ²),  all real x.

Don't memorise the constant; read the shape. The exponent is minus a squared distance from μ, in units of σ, so the curve peaks at μ and falls off fast and symmetrically. The constant 1/(σ√(2π)) is just whatever makes the area 1. Its mean is μ, its variance σ² (the parameters are named for what they are), and mean = median = mode = μ. The curve bends from "falling faster" to "falling slower" exactly at μ ± σ, the points of inflexion your corpus derives.

2 · One bell, many labels

Here is the fact that makes normals easy. If X ~ N(μ, σ²), then aX + b is normal too (L7 gives its mean and variance; Go deeper shows it stays normal). In particular, subtract the mean and divide by the SD:

Z = (X − μ)/σ ~ N(0, 1)

This is the standard normal distribution, and its distribution function has its own name, Φ(z) = P(Z ≤ z), tabulated in every statistics book. So every normal probability is one table lookup away. Watch what standardising really does, and predict first.

Say it in one line

Every normal is the standard bell, shifted to μ and stretched by σ. To find any normal probability, convert the cut points to z-scores, z = (x − μ)/σ, and read Φ. Symmetry fills the gaps: Φ(−z) = 1 − Φ(z).

3 · How much lies near the middle?

Since every normal is the same bell, "how much lies within k SDs of the mean" has one answer for all of them. Compare it with L7's guarantee for any shape.

"68–95–99.7." Within 1, 2 and 3 SDs of the mean, a normal holds about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of its probability. The three numbers are arbitrary facts of this one curve, so they're worth memorising as a string. Two exact companions the exam likes: 95% within ±1.96σ, and 50% within ±0.6745σ (the quartiles).

4 · Adding normals

If X and Y are independent normals, then any aX + bY is normal too (the reproductive property; Go deeper proves it with MGFs from L11). Its mean and variance follow L6 and L7, with one fact from L11's product rule: for independent X and Y the variances add, Var(aX + bY) = a²Var(X) + b²Var(Y). With a = 1 and b = −1:

X − Y ~ N(μX − μY, σX² + σY²)

"Var(X − Y) = Var(X) − Var(Y)." No: for independent X and Y the variances add even when you subtract, because (−1)² = 1. Take X and Y independent N(0, 1). The wrong rule says Var(X − Y) = 1 − 1 = 0, which would make X − Y a constant: two independent random numbers always differing by exactly the same amount. Absurd. The truth is Var(X − Y) = 2. Subtracting two uncertain things gives a more uncertain result, never a less uncertain one. A related slip: 2X and X1 + X2 (two independent copies) have the same mean, but variances 4σ² and 2σ²: doubling one draw doesn't average anything out.

WATCH OUT  In your corpus's proof of the reproductive property, the line before the conclusion writes the variance of l1X1 + l2X2 as l1σ1² + l2σ2², without squaring the l's; the next line has the correct l1²σ1² + l2²σ2². Two lines later "X2 − X2" should read X1 − X2. Both are in the scanned notes themselves, not OCR errors. Small slips, but Var(aX + bY) is a favourite exam question.

5 · Worked examples: marks

Marks in Paper 1 are X ~ N(60, 10²). (a) Find P(X > 75) and P(50 < X < 70). (b) Paper 2 marks are Y ~ N(50, 15²), independent of X. Find the chance a candidate scores more in Paper 1 than in Paper 2.

(a) Standardise: 75 is (75 − 60)/10 = 1.5 SDs above the mean, so

P(X > 75) = 1 − Φ(1.5) = 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668

And 50 to 70 is −1 to 1 in z-units: Φ(1) − Φ(−1) = 2Φ(1) − 1 = 0.6827, the "68" of the rule.

(b) "More in Paper 1" is X − Y > 0. The difference is normal with mean 60 − 50 = 10 and variance 100 + 225 = 325 (added!), so its SD is √325 = 18.03. Standardise the cut at 0:

P(X − Y > 0) = P(Z > −10/18.03) = Φ(0.5547) = 0.7105

Paper 1's higher average wins about 71% of the time, not always, because both papers are noisy.

In one or two sentences of your own: why can a single table, Φ, answer questions about every normal distribution?

Compare with a model answer

Because every normal is the standard normal shifted by μ and stretched by σ. Converting a cut point to z = (x − μ)/σ just relabels the axis; the area to one side doesn't change, so it can be read from the standard normal's table.

Your turn, half done. With marks X ~ N(60, 10²), find the mark that only the top 2.5% of candidates exceed.

Step 1: the top 2.5% of a standard normal starts at z = ____ (the "95% within ±…" number). Step 2: convert back: x = μ + zσ = 60 + ____ × 10 = ____ .

Check your steps

z = 1.96, since 95% lies within ±1.96 and the remaining 5% splits into 2.5% per tail. So x = 60 + 1.96 × 10 = 79.6.

The normal turns up everywhere for one reason: add up many small, independent effects, and the total looks normal, whatever each effect's own shape.

Many small effects, added upThe total that looks normal
tiny slips in a measuring processa measurement's error
many genes, meals and years of growthan adult's height
marks from many separate questionsan exam score
n fair coin flips (L8), for large nthe number of heads

The same fact is why a sample mean is treated as normal in every z-test of Units III–IV.

Its name, and where the normal leads

The fact is the central limit theorem. De Moivre found its first case in 1733: the bell inside the binomial (L8).

The sample mean's normal shape is behind confidence intervals too.

Gauss used the normal to model errors in astronomy. Least squares is exactly maximum likelihood when the errors are normal.

Squares of standard normals build the chi-square distribution (L14). Ratios of those build the t and F distributions, behind the t-test and ANOVA.

Among all distributions with a given variance, the normal has the largest entropy. That makes it the "least assuming" choice when only a mean and a variance are known.

Why this matters for you

EXAM  Expect: probabilities by standardising; the reverse ("find the cut-off for the top 10%"); 68–95–99.7 and ±1.96; sums and differences of independent normals; mean = median = mode, points of inflexion at μ ± σ; quartile deviation ≈ 0.6745σ and mean deviation σ√(2/π) ≈ 0.8σ; and the MGF eμt + σ²t²/2, often in "identify the distribution" form.

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

Show that the standard normal has MGF MZ(t) = et²/2, deduce MX(t) = eμt + σ²t²/2 for X ~ N(μ, σ²), and use it to prove the reproductive property.

Worked proof

Complete the square in the exponent: tz − z²/2 = t²/2 − (z − t)²/2. So

MZ(t) = et²/2 ∫ (1/√(2π)) e−(z−t)²/2 dz = et²/2

because the integral is the total area under a standard bell shifted to t, which is 1. With X = μ + σZ and L11's rule Ma+bZ(t) = eatMZ(bt):

MX(t) = eμteσ²t²/2

For independent normals, MaX+bY(t) = MX(at)MY(bt), and multiplying

eaμXt + a²σX²t²/2 × ebμYt + b²σY²t²/2

gives the normal barcode with mean aμX + bμY and variance a²σX² + b²σY². By uniqueness (L11), aX + bY is that normal. ∎

An ISI entrance problem

Here is a real ISI question, in plain words first. A continuous random number X has the MGF M(t) = et(t+1) for every t. What is the chance that X is at least 0? Write Φ for the standard normal distribution function. The options are (A) Φ(0), (B) Φ(−1/√2), (C) Φ(1/√2) and (D) Φ(1/2).

Source: ISI M.Stat 2020 objective paper (PSA), Q20. The paper asks for P(X ≥ 0).

Worked solution

Match the barcode, term by term:

ExponentNumber in front of tNumber in front of t²
given: t(t + 1) = t + t²11
normal: μt + σ²t²/2μσ²/2

So μ = 1 and σ²/2 = 1, giving σ² = 2. By uniqueness, X ~ N(1, 2).

Standardise the cut at 0 with σ = √2:

P(X ≥ 0) = P(Z ≥ −1/√2) = Φ(1/√2) ≈ 0.7602

using symmetry, P(Z ≥ −a) = Φ(a). Option (C).

The traps. Reading σ² = 1 from the t², forgetting the 1/2, leads to (D)-like answers. Dividing by σ² instead of σ gives Φ(1/2), which is (D).

Checked by script (practice bank, ISI checks, file psa-2020-q20). Numerical integration of e^(tx) against the N(1, 2) density matches e^(t² + t) at t = −1, 0.5, 1 and 2, to within 10⁻⁶. The numerical P(X ≥ 0) equals Φ(1/√2) to within 10⁻⁴.
