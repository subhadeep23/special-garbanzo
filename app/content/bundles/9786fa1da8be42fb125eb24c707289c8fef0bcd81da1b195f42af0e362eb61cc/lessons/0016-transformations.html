Transformations — the Distribution of g(X)

Probability is conserved; only its spacing changes. ~22 min.

TODAY'S WIN  Given the distribution of X, you'll be able to find the distribution of Y = g(X): by collecting masses when X is discrete, by the distribution-function ("CDF") method, which always works, and by the change-of-variable formula when g is monotone, including the fold that non-monotone functions need.

0 · Spaced recall — no peeking

• How do you get a density from a distribution function (L5)?

• χ²₂ is which exponential (L14)?

Answers: differentiate, f = F′ (L5). And χ²₂ = Gamma(1, rate 1/2), the exponential with mean 2 (L14). You'll meet it again in a minute, made from a uniform.

1 · Discrete: just move the masses

If X is discrete, each value x carries its mass to g(x), and values that land in the same place pool their mass:

P(Y = y) = Σ P(X = x)  over all x with g(x) = y.

Your corpus's example: X equally likely to be −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 or 3, and Y = X². Then Y = 0 gets 1/6, Y = 1 gets 2/6 (from ±1), Y = 4 gets 2/6 (from ±2) and Y = 9 gets 1/6 (from 3 alone). Nothing more to it.

2 · Continuous: the CDF method

For a continuous X the safe route always works: find FY(y) = P(g(X) ≤ y) by turning the event into one about X, then differentiate (L5). Take X ~ U(0, 1) and Y = −2 ln X. Since −2 ln x falls as x rises, −2 ln X ≤ y exactly when X ≥ e−y/2:

FY(y) = P(X ≥ e−y/2) = 1 − e−y/2,  y > 0.

That's the exponential with mean 2 (L12), which is χ²₂ (L14). From a single uniform number you've made a chi-square variable.

3 · Why densities change: the rubber ruler

Here's what the CDF method is doing underneath. Cut X's range into strips that each hold probability 0.1. The map g carries each strip to a new stretch of the y-axis, and the strip keeps its 0.1. If the new stretch is wider, the same probability is spread thinner and the density drops; if narrower, it piles up. Predict first.

Shrink the strips to width dx: a strip of probability fX(x) dx lands on width |dy|, so its new height is fX(x) |dx/dy|. That's the whole formula.

Say it in one line

For a strictly increasing or strictly decreasing g, with x = g−1(y), the change-of-variable formula is

fY(y) = fX(x) · |dx/dy|

Same probability, new spacing: |dx/dy| is how much the ruler was squeezed at that point.

Check it on Y = −2 ln X: x = e−y/2, |dx/dy| = ½e−y/2, and fX = 1, so fY(y) = ½e−y/2. Same answer as the CDF method.

A rubber ruler with sand on it. Spread sand on a rubber ruler (the density of X) and stretch the ruler unevenly (the map g). No sand is gained or lost, but where you stretch, the layer thins, and where you squeeze, it thickens. The thickness at each point is the old thickness times the squeeze factor |dx/dy|. The absolute value is there because flipping the ruler end for end doesn't make thickness negative.

Where it breaks: a rubber ruler can't fold back on itself, but a function like x² can: it sends x and −x to the same y, so two layers of sand land on one spot and must be added. And for a discrete X there's no layer to thin, just grains to move (section 1).

4 · Folding: when g isn't monotone

For Y = X² with X ~ U(−1, 1), every y in (0, 1) comes from two points, x = √y and x = −√y. Predict before step 3.

The CDF method handles the fold automatically, which is why it's the safe route:

FY(y) = P(−√y ≤ X ≤ √y) = √y  ⇒  fY(y) = 1/(2√y)

"Just put x = g−1(y) into fX." That forgets the squeeze factor. For Y = −2 ln X with X uniform, it would give fY(y) = fX(e−y/2) = 1 for every y > 0: a flat line over an infinite range, with infinite area, so not a density at all. The true answer, ½e−y/2, carries the factor |dx/dy|. The second classic slip is using the monotone formula on a function that folds, like x² on (−1, 1); it gives exactly half the correct density, as the picture shows. When unsure, use the CDF method.

5 · Worked examples

(a) X ~ U(0, 1). Find the density of Y = −2 ln X by the formula. (b) X ~ U(−1, 1). Find the density of Y = X² and P(Y ≤ 1/4). (c) X ~ N(μ, σ²) and Y = aX + b with a ≠ 0. Find the density of Y.

(a) g is decreasing, x = e−y/2, |dx/dy| = ½e−y/2, and fX(x) = 1 on (0, 1), which covers every y > 0. So fY(y) = ½e−y/2: χ²₂.

(b) By the CDF method above, fY(y) = 1/(2√y) on (0, 1), and

P(Y ≤ 1/4) = FY(1/4) = √(1/4) = 1/2

which is just P(|X| ≤ 1/2). The density is unbounded near 0 (squaring squeezes everything near 0 together) yet still has area 1, a reminder from L5 that densities may exceed 1.

(c) x = (y − b)/a, so |dx/dy| = 1/|a| and

fY(y) = fX((y − b)/a)/|a|

Substituting the normal density and simplifying gives the N(aμ + b, a²σ²) density: the "every normal is the same bell, shifted and stretched" fact of L13, now proved.

In one or two sentences of your own: why does the density of Y need the factor |dx/dy| while probabilities of matching events need no factor at all?

Compare with a model answer

Matching events hold the same probability, since a ≤ X ≤ b exactly when Y lands in the image interval. But a density is probability per unit length, and the map changes lengths, so the same probability over a new length needs the ratio of lengths, |dx/dy|.

The names, and two variables at once

Row two is the probability integral transform from your corpus, run backwards. It is called inverse-transform sampling. Row three is the Box–Muller transform. Row four gives the lognormal.

With two variables, the squeeze factor becomes a Jacobian determinant. That is how the gamma ratio X/(X + Y) was shown to be beta (L14), and how the t and F distributions are derived.

The delta method (Unit IV) approximates the mean and variance of g(X). It uses a local version of today's stretch factor.

Why this matters for you

EXAM  Expect: "if X ~ U(0, 1), find the distribution of −2 ln X" (χ²₂) or −ln X (Exp(1)); Y = X² for a symmetric X (the fold); aX + b for a normal; F(X) ~ U(0, 1) for continuous X; and discrete transformations by pooling masses. Many identify-the-distribution questions are transformations in disguise.

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

Prove the change-of-variable formula for strictly monotone, differentiable g, including the absolute value.

Worked proof

Increasing g. Then g(X) ≤ y exactly when X ≤ g−1(y), so FY(y) = FX(g−1(y)). Differentiate with the chain rule:

fY(y) = fX(g−1(y)) · dg−1(y)/dy

and the derivative is positive.

Decreasing g. Now g(X) ≤ y exactly when X ≥ g−1(y), so FY(y) = 1 − FX(g−1(y)) (using P(X = a) = 0, L5). Differentiating gives a minus sign times a negative derivative, which is positive:

fY(y) = −fX(g−1(y)) · dg−1(y)/dy

Both cases are fX(g−1(y)) |dg−1(y)/dy|. ∎ Your corpus proves it the same way, in two cases.

An ISI entrance problem

Here is a real ISI question, in plain words first. Pick a point (X, Y) evenly at random from the triangle with corners (−1, 0), (1, 0) and (0, 1). Find the density of |X| + Y.

Source: ISI M.Stat 2024 descriptive paper (PSB), Q4. In the paper's notation, the region is {(x, y) : 0 < y < 1, |x| < 1 − y}.

Worked solution

The region. At height y, x runs over (−(1 − y), 1 − y). That is a triangle with base 2 on the x-axis and apex (0, 1). Its area is 1, so the joint density is 1 on it (L15).

CDF method. Let W = |X| + Y, and fix 0 < w < 1. The set |x| + y ≤ w, with y > 0, is a smaller triangle: base from −w to w, apex (0, w). It lies inside the region, since |x| ≤ w − y < 1 − y. Its area is ½ × 2w × w = w².

So

P(W ≤ w) = w²  ⇒  fW(w) = 2w,  0 < w < 1.

That's Beta(2, 1) from L14, the same law as the larger of two uniforms. No Jacobian was needed: the CDF method turned a two-variable problem into an area.

Checked by script (practice bank, ISI checks, file psb-2024-q4, written for this lesson). Grid areas of {|x| + y ≤ w} inside the triangle match w² at w = 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 0.9, to within 0.001. Two million simulated points give E(W) = 0.6669, against the 2/3 that the density 2w implies.
