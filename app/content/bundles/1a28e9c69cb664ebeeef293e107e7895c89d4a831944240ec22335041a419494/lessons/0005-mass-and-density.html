Mass and Density — Discrete vs Continuous

Jumps hold probability. Smooth slopes spread it thin. ~18 min.

TODAY'S WIN  You'll be able to look at a distribution function and say whether it has a mass function or a density, get one from the other, check that a proposed density is valid, and explain the two facts that trip everyone up: a single point can have probability 0, and a density can be bigger than 1.

0 · Spaced recall — no peeking

• Three fair coin tosses, X = number of heads. What is P(X < 1), and where do you read it on the staircase?

• Told "the roll was odd", what happens to the bag of outcomes?

Answers: 1/8, the height just before the jump at 1 (the hollow dot). And the even faces leave the bag; the three odd faces share all the probability.

1 · Two ways probability can sit on a line

Last lesson's staircase rose only in jumps. Between jumps it was flat, so the number line held probability only at a few separate points: 0, 1, 2, 3 heads.

Now picture a different experiment: the time until the next bus, measured to any precision. There's no special list of values; the probability is smeared along the line. Its distribution function has no jumps at all. It climbs smoothly.

Those are the two pure cases, and each has its own description:

Distribution functionThe variable isDescribed by
rises only in jumpsdiscrete random variablea mass function: the jump sizes
rises smoothly, no jumpscontinuous random variablea density: the slope

A distribution function can also mix the two, part jumps and part slope. You met one in L4's exam problem. It has neither a pure mass function nor a pure density, so the exam checks you can spot it.

2 · Mass: the jump sizes

For a discrete variable, the probability mass function (p.m.f.) lists the probability at each point: p(x) = P(X = x). On the staircase it is simply the height of the jump at x:

p(x) = F(x) − F(x−)

Three coins: jumps of 1/8, 3/8, 3/8 and 1/8 at 0, 1, 2, 3. Two checks make any list a valid p.m.f., and they are the checks an exam question asks for:

1No value is negative: p(x) ≥ 0.
2They add to one: Σ p(x) = 1. All the probability is somewhere.

Going back is just adding: F(x) is the sum of p at every mass point up to and including x.

3 · Density: probability per unit length

Here's the question that makes continuous variables strange. Make a prediction before you step through the picture.

So for a continuous variable, P(X = a) = 0 for every single a. That isn't "impossible": the dart lands somewhere, and wherever it lands had probability 0. Probability lives on stretches, never on single points.

Then what can we say at a point? Not how much probability is there, but how tightly packed it is. Take a short stretch from x to x + h, find its probability, and divide by its length h. As h shrinks, that ratio settles on a number: the probability density function (p.d.f.), written f(x).

Say it in one line

Density is probability per unit length. Probability is area under the density: P(a < X ≤ b) = ∫ab f(x) dx, which is also F(b) − F(a). And f = F′ wherever F has a slope.

A metal rod of uneven thickness. Mass sits along the rod; the density at a point is mass per centimetre there. A thin slice has almost no mass, and a single cross-section has none, yet the density there is a perfectly good number. To get a piece's mass, you add up density × length along it, which is an integral. Your corpus uses the same picture: mass = density × volume.

Where it breaks: a real rod can weigh anything, but the total "mass" of a probability distribution is always exactly 1. And physical density has units (g/cm); probability density has units of 1/(units of x), which is why its size depends on how you measure x.

"Jump = mass, slope = density." Look at F. Where it jumps, the jump size is P(X = x). Where it slopes, the slope is f(x). Sum the masses; integrate the density.

"A density value is a probability, so it can't exceed 1." Wrong. Let X be uniform on (0, 1/2): equally likely anywhere in a stretch of length 1/2. The total area must be 1, so the height is 2. f(x) = 2, and nothing is broken, because no probability is ever read off a height alone. Only areas are probabilities, and every area here is at most 1. The same bank question appears in your Practice tab.

Two quick consequences you'll use constantly:

1For a continuous X, < and ≤ give the same answer: P(a < X < b) = P(a ≤ X ≤ b), because the endpoints carry no probability. (The staircase trap from L4 disappears.)
2A valid density needs only f(x) ≥ 0 and ∫ f(x) dx = 1 over the whole line. It may exceed 1; it may not dip below 0.

4 · Worked example: the triangle

Let f(x) = 1/2 − |x − 3|/4 for 1 ≤ x ≤ 5, and 0 elsewhere. (a) Show f is a density. (b) Find F. (c) Find P(2 < X < 4) and P(X = 3).

(a) f is never negative: it's 0 at x = 1 and x = 5 and rises to 1/2 at x = 3. Its graph is a triangle with base 4 and height 1/2, so its area is (1/2) × 4 × (1/2) = 1. Both checks pass.

(b) Left of the peak, f(x) = (x − 1)/4. The area from 1 to t is a small triangle, base t − 1, height (t − 1)/4:

F(t) = (t − 1)²/8  for 1 ≤ t ≤ 3,  and by symmetry  F(t) = 1 − (5 − t)²/8  for 3 ≤ t ≤ 5.

Check at the peak: F(3) = 4/8 = 1/2. Half the area lies left of the centre of a symmetric triangle, as it must.

(c) Subtract two readings of F:

P(2 < X < 4) = F(4) − F(2) = 7/8 − 1/8 = 3/4

And P(X = 3) = 0, even though 3 is where the density is highest: highest density means the most probability near 3, not at 3.

WATCH OUT  The corpus (Ex. 11) prints F(t) = t²/6 − t/4 + 1/6 on the left half. At t = 3 that gives 11/12, not 1/2, so it can't be right: half of a symmetric triangle's area must sit left of its peak. The correct piece is (t − 1)²/8. That's the fourth confirmed slip in this corpus. A one-point check like F(peak) = 1/2 catches it in seconds.

In one or two sentences of your own: why can the triangle's density reach 1/2, a uniform on (0, 1/2) reach 2, and neither break the rule that probabilities are at most 1?

Compare with a model answer

Because a density height is not a probability. Probabilities are areas under the curve, and the total area is always exactly 1, however tall and narrow the shape is.

Your turn, half done. For the same triangle, find P(X > 4.5).

Step 1: it's 1 − F(4.5). Step 2: 4.5 is right of the peak, so use F(t) = 1 − (5 − t)²/8. Step 3: 5 − 4.5 = ____, squared is ____, so the answer is ____ .

Check your steps

5 − 4.5 = 1/2, squared is 1/4, so P(X > 4.5) = (1/4)/8 = 1/32. That's the small corner triangle: base 1/2, height 1/8, area 1/32.

Every model in statistics is a mass function or a density. Real data reaches a density through the narrowing-bins picture, run backwards.

Say you time 20 phone calls and count how many fall in each 2-minute bin. Divide each count by 20 × 2. Then the bars' areas add to 1, just like a density's.

MinutesCallsBar height = calls ÷ (20 × 2)
0 to 290.225
2 to 460.15
4 to 630.075
6 to 820.05

Each height is a density estimate: chance per minute. The areas are 0.45, 0.3, 0.15 and 0.1, which add to 1.

Where this goes next

That bar chart is a histogram. Kernel density estimation smooths it into a curve.

In maximum likelihood (Unit IV) you multiply density values together. Densities above 1 are routine there, one more reason to be at ease with them.

Why are "sum for mass" and "integrate for density" one idea, not two? In measure theory, both are integrals against a measure. That unification, the Radon–Nikodym theorem, is what ISI's M.Stat probability course builds toward. You don't need it for the exam; you'll recognise it if you meet it.

Why this matters for you

Every named distribution in the next lessons is one of these two kinds of function.

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

Show that P(X = a) = F(a) − F(a−) for any random variable, where F(a−) is the limit of F from the left. Conclude that if F is continuous at a, then P(X = a) = 0.

Worked proof

The events An = {X ≤ a − 1/n} grow with n, and their union is {X < a}: any value below a is eventually below a − 1/n.

Probability is continuous along a growing sequence of events (this follows from the third axiom, countable additivity, applied to the rings An+1 − An). So

P(X < a) = lim P(An) = lim F(a − 1/n) = F(a−)

Then

P(X = a) = P(X ≤ a) − P(X < a) = F(a) − F(a−)

If F is continuous at a, the two are equal and the difference is 0. ∎

This is the proof behind "jump = mass". ISI's first-year probability course proves exactly this kind of continuity result before using it.

An ISI entrance problem

Here is a real ISI question, in plain words first. A phone call lasts a random X minutes. The company bills whole minutes, rounding up: a 22.09-minute call is billed as 23. Call the billed minutes T. What is the distribution of T, and is T discrete or continuous?

Source: ISI M.Stat 2024 descriptive paper (PSB), Q6, part one. In the paper's notation, X has density f(x) = λe−λx for x > 0, and T = ⌈X⌉.

Worked solution

T takes only the values 1, 2, 3, …, so it is discrete, even though X is continuous. Rounding piles each stretch of probability onto one point.

The call lastsBilled TChance
more than 0, up to 11F(1) − F(0)
more than 1, up to 22F(2) − F(1)
more than 2, up to 33F(3) − F(2)
more than k − 1, up to kkF(k) − F(k − 1)

The exponential has F(x) = 1 − e−λx, so

P(T = k) = F(k) − F(k − 1) = e−λ(k−1) − e−λk = (1 − q) qk−1,  with q = e−λ.

That's a geometric distribution (L10) with success chance 1 − e−λ. Check: the masses add to (1 − q)(1 + q + q² + …) = 1.

The full ISI question then asks for the maximum likelihood estimator of λ from n recorded calls. That is coming in Unit IV. The answer is log(T̄/(T̄ − 1)) when the average recorded length T̄ exceeds 1. A simulation with λ = 0.4 and 400,000 calls agreed with both the mass function and the estimator.
