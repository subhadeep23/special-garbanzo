Bernoulli and Binomial — Counting Successes

Four coins, and a bet on the "obvious" middle. ~24 min.

1 · A bet on the middle

The stall owner tosses four fair coins. "Pick a side," he says. "Exactly two heads, or anything else. Same prize either way."

Two heads out of four is the fair, middle result. It feels like the obvious pick.

Your puzzle · 3 minutes, pen and paper
  1. How many ways can four coins land? (Each coin has 2 sides.)
  2. Write out the ways that give exactly two heads. HHTT is one. How many are there?
  3. So what is the chance of exactly two heads? More or less than a half?

Voice A: "Two heads is the middle, the most likely count. Pick it."

Voice B: "There are five possible counts: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4. Most likely isn't the same as more likely than all the rest put together."

Write down your side, and the chance you worked out.

2 · One trial: Bernoulli

Start with the smallest possible experiment: one try that either works or doesn't. You guess one multiple-choice question with four options. Right or wrong; nothing else.

A Bernoulli trial is a single try with exactly two outcomes, called success and failure, with success chance p. Write q = 1 − p for the failure chance. Turn it into a number: X = 1 for success, X = 0 for failure. Then

E[X] = 1·p + 0·q = p  and, since X² = X,  Var(X) = p − p² = pq

For a guessed question, p = 1/4: the mean is 1/4 of a right answer, and the variance is (1/4)(3/4) = 3/16. Both are L6 and L7, applied to the simplest variable there is.

3 · Many trials: count the successes

Now guess four questions, independently, each right with chance 1/4. Let X be the number you get right. What is P(X = 2)?

Take one particular pattern first: right, right, wrong, wrong. The guesses are independent, so multiply: (1/4)(1/4)(3/4)(3/4) = 9/256. But "two right" can happen in other patterns too. Every pattern with two rights and two wrongs has the same chance, 9/256, because it multiplies the same four numbers in a different order. So the only real question is how many patterns there are. List them:

RRWW  RWRW  RWWR  WRRW  WRWR  WWRR

Six. So P(X = 2) = 6 × 9/256 = 54/256. That's the whole method: count the patterns, price one pattern, multiply. Step through all five counts, and predict first.

Counting patterns without listing them

Listing works for four questions; for twenty it doesn't. A pattern is fixed once you say which x of the n trials succeeded. Choose them one at a time: n ways for the first, n − 1 for the second, and so on, x times. But that counts each set of positions once for every order you could have picked it in, and x positions can be picked in x! = x(x − 1)…1 orders. Divide them out:

C(n, x) = n(n − 1)…(n − x + 1)/x! = n!/(x!(n − x)!)

read "n choose x". Check: C(4, 2) = (4 × 3)/(2 × 1) = 6, the six patterns above.

Back to the stall: Voice B wins. Four coins land 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 ways, all equally likely. Exactly two heads covers C(4, 2) = 6 of them, so its chance is 6/16 = 3/8. "Anything else" gets the other 10/16. Two heads is the single most likely count, but the other four counts together beat it. Pick "anything else".

Tickets that all cost the same. Think of each pattern (like RRWW) as a ticket to the result "x successes". Every ticket for the same x costs exactly the same, so the total is just count × price.

Buying ticketsBinomial
one ticketone pattern of successes and failures

how many ticketsC(n, x)
total spendP(X = x)

Where it breaks: real prices add up to any amount; here every "price" is a chance, and all the tickets for all values of x together cost exactly 1. And the tickets must be different patterns: counting one pattern twice is the classic way to get a binomial wrong.

Say it in one line

If X counts the successes in n independent trials, each with success chance p, then X has the binomial distribution, written X ~ Bin(n, p):

P(X = x) = C(n, x) px qn−x,  x = 0, 1, …, n

Patterns, times the chance of one pattern.

BINS: the four checks before you use the formula. Binary outcome on each trial; Independent trials; Number of trials fixed in advance; Same success chance every time. Any one missing and the count is not binomial. (The list is arbitrary to remember, so a mnemonic earns its place here.)

4 · Where it balances, how much it spreads

The count X is a sum of n Bernoulli variables, one per trial (1 if that trial succeeds). By linearity (L6), the means add: E[X] = p + p + … + p = np. For independent trials the variances add too, giving

E[X] = np   and   Var(X) = npq

(Why variances add for independent trials is proved properly in L15; the Go-deeper block below gets npq straight from the formula instead, using only today's tools.) Play with n and p and watch the balance point.

One consequence the exam loves: npq < np whenever p > 0, because q is below 1. A binomial's variance is always smaller than its mean. And since pq ≤ 1/4 (largest at p = 1/2), Var(X) ≤ n/4.

"Any count of successes is binomial." No: the trials must be independent with the same p. Draw 2 balls without replacement from an urn of 2 red and 2 blue. The chance both are red is (2/4)(1/3) = 1/6, because the first draw changes the urn (L2's urn picture). The binomial Bin(2, 1/2) would say (1/2)² = 1/4, which is wrong. Without replacement, the count follows a different law (the hypergeometric, later in Unit I). With replacement, or from a huge population, binomial is right or nearly right. The other classic slip is forgetting the pattern count C(n, x) and writing just pxqn−x, the chance of one pattern.

5 · Worked examples: guessing an exam

(a) You guess all 10 questions of a quiz, 4 options each. Find the chance of at least 2 right, and the mean and variance of the number right. (b) A binomial variable has mean 4 and variance 3. Find n, p and P(X = 0).

(a) BINS holds: right/wrong, independent guesses, n = 10 fixed, p = 1/4 each time. So X ~ Bin(10, 1/4). "At least 2" has nine cases; its complement has two. Use the complement:

P(X = 0) = (3/4)10 = 0.0563

P(X = 1) = 10 (1/4)(3/4)9 = 0.1877

P(X ≥ 2) = 1 − 0.0563 − 0.1877 = 0.7560

Mean np = 10 × 1/4 = 2.5; variance npq = 10 × 1/4 × 3/4 = 1.875. (Check: 1.875 < 2.5, as every binomial's must be.)

(b) Divide the variance by the mean: npq/np = q = 3/4. So p = 1/4, and n = 4/(1/4) = 16. Then

P(X = 0) = (3/4)16 = 0.0100

If the "variance" had come out bigger than the mean, q would exceed 1 and no binomial could fit. Spotting that is a standard exam mark.

In one or two sentences of your own: why does every pattern with x successes in n trials have the same chance, and why does that make the formula a product of two parts?

Compare with a model answer

Each pattern multiplies p once per success and q once per failure, so any pattern with x successes multiplies the same numbers, pxqn−x, just in another order. Since all those patterns are separate (disjoint) ways for X = x to happen, their chances add, and adding the same number C(n, x) times is multiplying.

Your turn, half done. A binomial variable has mean 6 and variance 2.4. Find n and p.

Step 1: q = npq/np = 2.4/6 = ____, so p = ____ . Step 2: n = 6/p = ____ .

Check your steps

q = 0.4, so p = 0.6, and n = 6/0.6 = 10. Check: npq = 10 × 0.6 × 0.4 = 2.4 ✓.

Your pocket question · carry it for a few days

Toss 100 fair coins. The most likely count is 50 heads, yet its chance is only about 8%. Toss 10,000 coins: the chance of exactly 5,000 heads is under 1%. The "most likely" result keeps getting less likely as you toss more. Yet everyone says the share of heads gets closer to a half. How can both be true?

Hint 1 (after a day)

Work out the SD of the count, √(npq), for 100 coins and for 10,000 coins. Over roughly how many different counts is most of the chance spread in each case?

Hint 2 (after another day)

Now look at the share of heads, X/n, instead of the count. Use L7's rule Var(aX) = a²Var(X) with a = 1/n. What happens to its SD as n grows?

The answer (try for three days first)

The count's SD is 5 for 100 coins and 50 for 10,000. So the chance spreads over more and more counts, and each single count, even the middle one, gets a thinner slice. But the share X/n has SD √(pq/n): 0.05 for 100 coins, 0.005 for 10,000. The count wanders further from n/2 in absolute terms, while the share hugs 1/2 ever more tightly. Both are true at once. The first fact grows into the normal curve (L13); the second is the law of large numbers.

The binomial is the model behind every proportion you'll ever estimate. Each response is one trial, and the count of "yes" answers is binomial.

WhereOne trial, and its successThe binomial count
Opinion pollask one person; success = "yes"yes answers among n people
Factory checktest one item; success = faultyfaulty items in a batch of n
Websiteone visit; success = a click on the adclicks in n visits

This holds when you sample with replacement, or from a population much larger than n.

Where the binomial goes next

Logistic regression models each outcome as a Bernoulli trial with its own p.

The binomial test and the sign test (Unit V, nonparametrics) use the binomial directly.

De Moivre proved in 1733 that Bin(n, p) looks like a bell curve for large n. That was the first central limit theorem ever proved. It is why proportions get normal-based confidence intervals.

Sampling without replacement from a small population changes the model: the hypergeometric takes over. Survey sampling (Unit III) corrects the variance with the finite population factor.

Why this matters for you

Also: the most likely value sits near (n + 1)p, and Var(X) ≤ n/4. In UGC-NET the binomial is the stepping stone to the Poisson (next lesson) and the normal approximation.

Go deeper — one proof, one ISI problem (optional, ~15 min)

Prove it

For X ~ Bin(n, p), derive E[X] = np and Var(X) = npq straight from the mass function, without assuming that variances add.

Worked proof

A counting identity. x C(n, x) = n C(n − 1, x − 1). Both sides count the ways to pick a team of x from n people and then a captain from the team: left, team first then captain; right, captain first (n ways) then the other x − 1 from the remaining n − 1.

Mean. The x = 0 term is zero, so

E[X] = Σ x C(n, x) pxqn−x = np Σ C(n − 1, x − 1) px−1qn−x

The last sum runs over all the probabilities of Bin(n − 1, p), so it equals 1. Hence E[X] = np.

Variance. Apply the identity twice:

x(x − 1) C(n, x) = n(n − 1) C(n − 2, x − 2)

The same argument, now with all of Bin(n − 2, p) summing to 1, gives

E[X(X − 1)] = n(n − 1)p²

Then by L7's shortcut, since E[X²] = E[X(X − 1)] + E[X],

Var(X) = n(n − 1)p² + np − n²p² = np − np² = npq ∎

Your corpus does exactly this with "factorial moments" E[X(X − 1)…(X − r + 1)]; the general one is n(n − 1)…(n − r + 1)pr.

An ISI entrance problem

Here is a real ISI question, in plain words first. You hand out r different books at random among n children, where n is at least 3. For each j from 0 to r, what is the chance that the first child gets exactly j books?

Source: ISI M.Stat 2022 descriptive paper (PSB), Q5(a). In the paper's notation: r distinct books, n ≥ 3 children, and j = 0, 1, …, r.

Worked solution

"At random" means each book goes to one of the n children, each equally likely, independently of the other books. Now look only at child 1, and run BINS over the books:

BINS checkHere
Binary outcomeeach book goes to child 1, or it doesn't
Independent trialsbooks are placed independently
Number fixedthere are r books
Same chanceeach book goes to child 1 with chance 1/n

So the number child 1 gets is Bin(r, 1/n):

P(child 1 gets j) = C(r, j) (1/n)j (1 − 1/n)r−j

Child 1 expects r/n books, a fair share, as it should be.

One step further (toward part (b) of the question): how many children get no book, on average? Child i gets none with chance (1 − 1/n)r, the j = 0 case above.

Count 1 for each child with no book. The total is a sum of n such 0-or-1 counts. So by linearity (L6), its mean is n(1 − 1/n)r.

No independence is needed. The children's fates are not independent, since the books must go somewhere, and linearity doesn't care.

With r = 4 books and n = 5 children, child 1 gets nothing with chance (4/5)4 = 256/625. On average, 5 × 256/625 = 2.048 children go without.

Checked by script. The practice bank's ISI check (file psb-2022-q5) lists every assignment for (r, n) = (4, 5) and (6, 3). It confirms child 1's count is exactly Bin(r, 1/n). The lesson's own check lists all 625 assignments for r = 4, n = 5, and gets 256/625 and 2.048.
