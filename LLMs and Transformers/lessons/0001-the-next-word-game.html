LLMs & Transformers · Part I: Counting · Lesson 1

The next-word game

Every model you have ever typed a prompt into is playing one game: guess the next word. The game is more than a hundred years old. Today you build the first version of it, by counting.

Where we are in time 1913 — Andrei Markov counts letters in a Russian poem. 1948 — Claude Shannon uses the same idea to generate fake English. Nothing here needs a computer. Keep that in mind: the only thing that changes between this lesson and ChatGPT is how the guessing table gets built. → Course timeline

1 · The game

Finish this: "I'd like a cup of ___." You said tea or coffee without thinking. You didn't pick one word — you had a sense of which words were likely and which were absurd. "A cup of bicycle" is not wrong grammar. It's just very unlikely.

A language model is anything that turns "the words so far" into "how likely each possible next word is." That is the whole definition. Everything in this course is a better way of doing it.

🗣️ Plain: a machine that, given the words so far, says how likely each next word is. STATa conditional probability distribution over the next word, given the preceding words
CS/MLlanguage model (LM); an autoregressive LM generates by repeatedly sampling from it

2 · 1913: Markov counts vowels

Markov took the first 20,000 letters of Pushkin's Eugene Onegin, stripped the spaces and punctuation, and sorted every letter into just two kinds: vowel or consonant. By hand. He found 8,638 vowels — about 43%.

If each letter ignored the one before it, two vowels in a row would come up about 0.43 × 0.43 ≈ 19% of the time: roughly 3,731 double vowels. He counted 1,104. More than three times fewer.

So letters are not independent. What comes next depends on what came just before — after a vowel, another vowel is rare (roughly 1,104 ÷ 8,638 ≈ 0.13). That "depends on the previous one" chain now carries his name.

🗣️ Plain: the next thing depends on the thing just before it — and on nothing earlier. STATa first-order Markov chain; the test he ran is a check of independence between successive trials
CS/MLthe Markov assumption

3 · 1948: Shannon generates fake English

Thirty-five years later, Claude Shannon asked the reverse question. If I know how often each letter or word follows another, can I produce text? He built a ladder, each rung looking a little further back. Real samples from his paper:

Rung What it knows Shannon's sample
letters, zero ordernothing — every letter equally likely XFOML RXKHRJFFJUJ ZLPWCFWKCYJ
letters, looking back 1which letter tends to follow which ON IE ANTSOUTINYS ARE T INCTORE ST BE S DEAMY
words, looking back 1which word tends to follow which THE HEAD AND IN FRONTAL ATTACK ON AN ENGLISH WRITER THAT THE CHARACTER OF THIS POINT IS THEREFORE ANOTHER METHOD FOR THE LETTERS…

Each rung reads more like English. Shannon's own observation: the text holds together for about twice the distance the model looks back. And his method needed no tables — for the word samples he opened a book at random, found the current word, and wrote down whatever came after it. Then did it again on another page.

"It appears then that a sufficiently complex stochastic process will give a satisfactory representation of a discrete source." — Shannon, 1948

Read that sentence with 2026 eyes. It is the bet the whole field made.

4 · The maths, in two steps

Step 1 — forget most of the past

Picture it You're guessing the next word, but you have terrible short-term memory: you can only see the single word right before the blank. "…so beautifully ___". You still do better than random — beautifully narrows things down a lot.
P( next word | all words so far ) ≈ P( next word | previous word )
  • P( … )"the probability of …"
  • |"given that we know …" — the bar from conditional probability
  • ≈"is approximately" — we're choosing to throw information away
  • left sidewhat we'd like: use the whole history
  • right sidewhat we settle for: use only the last word

That's the Markov assumption, applied to words. A model that looks back one word is a bigram model ("bi" = two words: the previous one and the next one). Looking back two words is a trigram. In general, an n-gram looks back n − 1 words.

Step 2 — get the numbers by counting

Picture it Keep a notebook with one page for every word. Read a big pile of text. Every time you see a word, turn to its page and tally whatever word came right after it. To guess what follows like, open the like page: each word's share of the tallies is its chance.
P( next | previous ) = count( previous, next ) ÷ count( previous )
  • count( previous, next )how many times the pair appeared side by side, in that order
  • count( previous )how many times the previous word appeared with something after it — the total tallies on its page
  • ÷turns a tally into a share, so the shares on one page add up to 1
  • =this is not an approximation any more: it's the estimate we'll use
🗣️ Plain: the chance of a pair is its share of all the times the first word showed up. STATthe relative-frequency estimate of a conditional probability — which is the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) for this model. Your old friend.
CS/MLn-gram language model, trained by counting (Jurafsky & Martin, eq. 3.10)

Try it by hand first

The whole "text" is: i like tea . i like cake . you like tea .
What is P( tea | like )? And P( like | i )?

Show the answer

like appears 3 times, followed by tea twice and cake once. So P(tea | like) = 2 ÷ 3 ≈ 0.67. i appears twice, both times followed by like: P(like | i) = 2 ÷ 2 = 1. The model is certain — because it has seen almost nothing.

5 · Now build one

Below is a real bigram model, running in your browser. The text is short on purpose so you can check its counts. Do these three things, in order:

  1. Type the in the context box. Pick one row and check its division yourself.
  2. Switch between unigram, bigram, and trigram, pressing Generate a few times each. You are climbing Shannon's ladder.
  3. Paste in any paragraph you like — an email, a news article — and repeat. Try a context word that isn't in the text.

What you should notice: the unigram output is word salad. The bigram output has locally sensible pairs (the cat, sat on) but wanders. The trigram output on this tiny text mostly copies whole sentences — it has seen too little to do anything else.

6 · What's wrong with it — and what each flaw started

Every later chapter of this course is a response to one of these. Hold on to the list.

Flaw What you saw The response
Zeros an unseen pair gets probability 0 — "impossible", not "rare" smoothing (Lesson 2)
Short memory "The keys I left on the kitchen table are…" — the verb depends on a word six back recurrent networks, then attention (Parts III–IV)
Explosion with 50,000 words, a trigram model has 50,000 × 50,000 = 2.5 billion possible contexts — almost all never seen neural models that compute probabilities instead of storing them (Lesson 3)
No meaning learning about cat teaches the model nothing about dog — they're unrelated symbols words as vectors (Lessons 3–4)
The thread to today A modern LLM still outputs P( next token | previous tokens ). Two things changed: "previous" grew from one word to hundreds of thousands of tokens, and the notebook of counts was replaced by a neural network that computes the table on demand. Karpathy's build-GPT video starts from a bigram model too — a character-level one, with the counting replaced by a tiny neural network.
