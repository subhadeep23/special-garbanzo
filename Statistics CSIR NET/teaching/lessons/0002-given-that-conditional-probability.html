"Given that…" — Conditional Probability

What happens to a probability when you learn something new. Still plain language. ~12 min.

1 · The plain idea: news shrinks the bag

Roll a fair die. The chance of a 3 is 1 in 6.

Now someone tells you: "the result was an odd number." Nothing about the die changed — but your knowledge did. Odd faces are {1, 3, 5}. The other three faces are now impossible, so they leave the picture entirely.

You are no longer choosing from six equally likely faces. You are choosing from three. So the chance of a 3 is now 1 in 3.

The whole idea in one sentence

New information does not change the world — it shrinks the sample space down to the outcomes still consistent with what you were told, and you re-do the count inside that smaller bag.

That is conditional probability. "Conditional" simply means on the condition that we already know something. We write it with a vertical bar:

P(B | A)  =  "the probability of B, given that A has happened"

Read the bar out loud as the word "given". P(3 | odd) is just "chance of a 3, given it was odd". The bar is not division and it is not "or" — it is the word "given".

2 · Where the formula comes from

Let's redo the die example slowly, and the formula will appear by itself.

Being told A ("odd") means we only care about the part of the world where A is true. Inside that part, we want the outcomes where B ("a 3") is also true. So we need two counts:

What we needIn the die exampleAs a probability
outcomes where A and B both holdjust {3}P(A ∩ B) = 1/6
outcomes where A holds at all{1, 3, 5}P(A) = 3/6

The answer is the first as a share of the second: (1/6) ÷ (3/6) = 1/3. Which is exactly what we said in plain words. So:

P(B | A) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(A)

defined whenever P(A) > 0 — you cannot condition on something impossible

Two things to notice, because both are marks in an exam:

1The symbol ∩ means "and" — both events happen together. Many books write it as just P(AB), with no symbol at all. Same thing.
2Dividing by P(A) is the re-normalising step. The old bag totalled 1; the new smaller bag only totalled P(A). Dividing scales it back up so the new bag totals 1 again.

3 · A worked example — do this one with me

Two fair dice are thrown. Given that the total is divisible by 5, what is the chance that both dice show a five?

Take it in three steps, and never skip the first one.

Step 1 — name the events. Let A = "the total is divisible by 5". Let B = "both dice show five".

Step 2 — find P(A). Out of 36 equally likely pairs, which totals are divisible by 5? Totals of 5 and of 10. Total 5 happens 4 ways (1-4, 2-3, 3-2, 4-1); total 10 happens 3 ways (4-6, 5-5, 6-4). That is 7 pairs, so P(A) = 7/36.

Step 3 — find P(A ∩ B). "Both show five" is the single pair 5-5, and its total is 10, which is divisible by 5. So A and B hold together in exactly 1 of 36 cases: P(A ∩ B) = 1/36.

Answer. P(B | A) = (1/36) ÷ (7/36) = 1/7.

Notice the shape of the work. The 36 cancelled. Conditional probability questions are, almost always, counting inside the smaller bag — here, 1 good case out of the 7 cases that survived the news.

4 · Read the formula backwards: the multiplication rule

Move P(A) to the other side and you get a second tool, which is used even more often than the first:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A)

In words: the chance both happen = the chance the first happens, times the chance the second happens once the first already has. This is how you handle anything drawn without replacement, where each draw changes what is left.

Example. An urn holds 6 red and 4 black balls. Two are drawn without replacement. What is the chance both are red?

First ball red: 6/10. Now 9 balls remain and only 5 are red, so second ball red given the first was red: 5/9.

P(both red) = (6/10) × (5/9) = 30/90 = 1/3.

The rule extends as far as you need, one factor per draw, each conditioned on everything before it:

P(A1 ∩ A2 ∩ A3) = P(A1) × P(A2 | A1) × P(A3 | A1 ∩ A2)

This is called the chain rule (or multiplication theorem). It holds as long as every event you condition on has non-zero probability.

5 · Independence — when the news changes nothing

Sometimes you learn A, and your view of B does not move at all. Toss a coin twice: knowing the first toss was heads tells you nothing about the second. In symbols, P(B | A) = P(B).

Put that into the multiplication rule and it simplifies beautifully:

A and B are independent  ⇔  P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)

That product form is the definition examiners use, because it stays valid even when P(A) = 0 and the "given" version would break.

The trap that costs marks — read this twice

Independent and mutually exclusive are not the same thing. They are close to opposites.

  • Mutually exclusive means they cannot both happen: P(A ∩ B) = 0. So learning A happened tells you B definitely did not — that is a huge amount of information.
  • Independent means learning A tells you nothing about B.

So two events with real probabilities that are mutually exclusive are, for that very reason, not independent. Check: if they were independent, P(A)P(B) would have to equal 0, which forces one of them to be impossible.

6 · Optional deeper layer — skip this on a busy week

Only if you're curious today. A standard theory question asks you to show that conditional probability is itself a probability. The point is that P(· | A) obeys the same three rules from Lesson 1:

  • Never negative — it's a non-negative number divided by a positive one.
  • Totals one — the whole sample space, given A, is just A itself, and P(A) ÷ P(A) = 1.
  • Separate things add — if events don't overlap, neither do their overlaps with A, so the additions carry through the division unchanged.

The consequence is the useful part: every rule you already know stays true after conditioning. The complement rule still works, so P(not B | A) = 1 − P(B | A). You are not learning a new system — you are working in the old one, in a smaller bag.

Why this matters for you
