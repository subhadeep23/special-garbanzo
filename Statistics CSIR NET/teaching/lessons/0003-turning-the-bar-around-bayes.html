Turning the Bar Around — Bayes' Theorem

You know the chance of the symptom given the disease. You want the disease given the symptom. ~15 min.

TODAY'S WIN  By the end you'll be able to run a full Bayes calculation on the standard exam shape — several causes, one observed effect — and explain why a positive test for a rare disease usually means far less than people think.

0 · Spaced recall — no peeking

Two questions from Lesson 2. Answer them in your head before reading on. If you get them slowly, that is fine; slow retrieval still strengthens the memory.

• Write the multiplication rule for P(A ∩ B).

• A bag has 4 red and 6 blue balls. Two drawn without replacement. Chance both red?

Answers: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B|A); and (4/10) × (3/9) = 12/90 = 2/15.

1 · The problem Bayes solves

Almost every real question has an awkward shape. You are handed the probabilities running one way, and asked for the probability running the other way.

What you are givenWhat you are asked
P(defective | made by machine 3)P(made by machine 3 | defective)
P(test positive | you have the disease)P(you have the disease | test positive)
P(this evidence | the suspect did it)P(the suspect did it | this evidence)

Look at the left column. In every row it is a cause on the right of the bar and an effect on the left. That direction is the easy one — a factory can measure its own defect rate; a lab can measure how often the test fires on known patients.

The right column is what you actually want, and it runs backwards: effect known, cause unknown. Bayes' theorem is the tool that turns the bar around.

Say it in one line

Bayes' theorem converts P(effect | cause), which is measurable, into P(cause | effect), which is what you actually want to know.

2 · First, the denominator: total probability

Before Bayes, you need one smaller idea, and it is worth meeting on its own — the exam asks for it by name.

Suppose an effect A can only arrive through one of several routes B1, B2, …, Bk. Those routes must be:

1Mutually exclusive — no two can happen together. A pen came from machine 1 or 2 or 3, never two at once.
2Exhaustive — together they cover everything. Every pen came from some machine; there is no fourth option.

A family of events with both properties is called a partition of the sample space. Keep the word — it is the condition every Bayes question quietly assumes.

Then the total chance of A is just: chance of each route, times the chance A happens along that route, all added up.

P(A) = Σi P(Bi) · P(A | Bi)

the theorem of total probability — each term is one branch of the multiplication rule

Notice there is nothing new here. Each term P(Bi)P(A|Bi) is exactly P(Bi ∩ A) from Lesson 2's multiplication rule. Because the routes don't overlap, we are allowed to add them (Lesson 1, axiom 3). Two old rules, stacked.

3 · Now Bayes

You want P(Bi | A). Start from the definition in Lesson 2 and substitute twice:

Definition: P(Bi | A) = P(Bi ∩ A) ÷ P(A)

Top, by the multiplication rule: P(Bi ∩ A) = P(Bi) · P(A | Bi)

Bottom, by total probability: P(A) = Σj P(Bj) · P(A | Bj)

P(Bi | A) = P(Bi) · P(A | Bi) ÷ Σj P(Bj) · P(A | Bj)

That is the whole theorem, and you just derived it in three lines from things you already had. There is no new machinery in Bayes at all — it is the definition of conditional probability with the top and bottom rewritten.

Read the formula as a sentence

Numerator: the weight of the route you're asking about.
Denominator: the total weight of all routes that could have produced what you saw.
So the posterior is simply your route's share of the total. If that clicks, you never have to memorise the formula — every Bayes question is "compute all the branch weights, then take one branch's share."

4 · The vocabulary: prior and posterior

Two terms the exam uses freely, both of which the corpus states in Latin:

TermAlso writtenMeans
Prior probabilitya priori, P(Bi)your belief in each cause before seeing the evidence
LikelihoodP(A | Bi)how well each cause explains what you saw
Posterior probabilitya posteriori, P(Bi | A)your updated belief after the evidence arrived

So Bayes is prior × likelihood, then normalise. Hold on to the word likelihood — in Unit IV it becomes the whole basis of estimation, and it will be exactly this same object.

5 · The standard exam problem, worked in full

A factory has three machines making pens. Machine 1 makes 35% of all pens, machine 2 makes 25%, machine 3 makes 40%. Their defect rates are 2%, 1% and 3% respectively.

(a) A pen is picked at random. What is the chance it is defective?
(b) Given that it is defective, what is the chance machine 3 made it?

Step 1 — name the events and check the partition. Let Ai = "made by machine i", and B = "defective". The three machines are mutually exclusive and exhaustive (0.35 + 0.25 + 0.40 = 1 ✓). Good — Bayes applies.

Step 2 — build the branch table. This table is the entire method. Build it every time.

Machine iPrior P(Ai)Likelihood P(B|Ai)Branch weight P(Ai)P(B|Ai)
10.350.020.0070
20.250.010.0025
30.400.030.0120
total1.00—0.0215

Answer (a). The last cell already is it: P(B) = 0.0215. That is the theorem of total probability, done as a column sum.

Answer (b). Machine 3's share of the total:

P(A3 | B) = 0.0120 ÷ 0.0215 = 120/215 = 24/43 ≈ 0.558

Look at what moved

Machine 3's prior was 0.40. After learning the pen is defective, its posterior is 0.558. The evidence pushed suspicion towards machine 3 — correctly, since it is the sloppiest machine. That shift, prior → posterior, is what Bayes computes. Always sanity-check the direction of the shift; if it moved the wrong way, you have swapped a row.

WATCH OUT  The corpus version of this problem has two OCR typos in its working: it prints 0.035 for 0.35 and 0.08 for 0.03. The final answers, 0.0215 and 120/215, are correct. This is a live example of why you should re-derive every printed number rather than trusting it — a habit that also catches your own slips in the exam hall.

6 · The trap worth more than the formula: base rates

Here is where Bayes stops being arithmetic and starts being judgement.

A disease affects 1 in 1000 people. A test is very good: it catches 99% of people who have it, and gives a false alarm on only 5% of healthy people.

You test positive. What is the chance you have the disease?

Most people say "about 95%". Build the table instead. Imagine 100,000 people.

GroupHow manyTest positive ratePositives
Has the disease1000.9999
Healthy99,9000.054,995
total positives——5,094

P(disease | positive) = 99 ÷ 5094 ≈ 0.019

Under 2%. The test is genuinely good, and the answer is still tiny — because healthy people are so much more numerous that their 5% false alarms swamp the 99 true positives. The prior (also called the base rate) does most of the work, and ignoring it is the single most common mistake made with Bayes.

Note also what the evidence did do: your chance went from 1 in 1000 (0.001) to about 1 in 51 (0.019) — a nineteen-fold increase. The test was informative. It just started from a very low base.

Why this matters for you

EXAM  Bayes and total probability are near-guaranteed marks in UGC-NET 107 Unit I, and they usually appear in exactly the three-machines shape: several sources, one observed defect, find the posterior. Beyond Unit I, the prior–likelihood–posterior triple is the backbone of Bayesian estimation in Unit IV, and "likelihood" in maximum likelihood estimation is the same P(data | parameter) you met in the middle column today. Learn the branch table once; reuse it for the rest of the syllabus.
