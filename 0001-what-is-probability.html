What Probability Really Means

Starting from zero, in plain language. No formulas until we actually need them. ~10 min.

TODAY'S WIN  By the end you'll be able to say, in your own words, what probability is, and the three simple rules every probability must follow — using three new words you'll meet gently along the way.

1 · The plain idea

Probability is just a way of putting a number on how likely something is.

We agree on a scale from 0 to 1:

• 0 means "this never happens."

• 1 means "this always happens, for sure."

• 0.5 means "it's a coin toss — half the time."

That's the whole heart of it. A weather app saying "70% chance of rain" is just saying the probability of rain is 0.7 — closer to the "sure" end than the "never" end. Everything else in this subject is built on top of this one simple idea.

2 · Three words, introduced gently

To talk about likelihood precisely, we need three small words. You already understand all three from daily life — we're just giving them their proper names.

Word 1 — outcome

An outcome is one single thing that could happen. Roll a die: getting a 4 is one outcome. Getting a 6 is another.

Word 2 — sample space

Now imagine a bag holding every outcome that's possible. That whole collection is the sample space. For a die, the sample space is the six faces: {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Nothing outside the bag can happen; everything inside might.

Think of it as the complete "list of everything that could occur." Later you'll see it written with the symbol \(\Omega\) (the Greek letter omega) — but the symbol is just shorthand for "the bag of all outcomes." Don't let the symbol worry you.

Word 3 — event

An event is a question you can answer yes-or-no about the result — usually a group of outcomes you care about. "Did the die show an even number?" is an event. It's happy with three outcomes: 2, 4, or 6. So an event is simply a chosen bunch of outcomes from the bag.

3 · The three rules probability must obey

Whatever numbers we hand out as probabilities, they have to make common sense. It turns out three simple rules are enough to keep everything honest. Here they are in plain words first:

1No negatives. A likelihood can't be less than zero. The smallest allowed is 0 ("never").
2Something must happen. The probability that some outcome in the bag occurs is exactly 1. (The die shows a face — guaranteed.)
3Separate things add up. If two events can't happen together, the chance of "one or the other" is just their chances added. Chance of rolling a 2 or a 5 = chance of 2 + chance of 5.

That's it. These three are called the axioms of probability — "axiom" is just the academic word for a starting rule we agree to, and build everything else on. They were written down in this clean form by a Russian mathematician, Kolmogorov, in 1933. You don't need his machinery today; you just need the three common-sense rules above.

Why this matters for you

Almost every result you'll meet later — adding probabilities, "at least one", complements — is squeezed out of these three rules. So if a formula ever slips your mind in the exam, you can often rebuild it from these three sentences. That's the real payoff of starting here: fewer things to memorize, more things you can reconstruct.

4 · One tiny result, to see the rules working

Here's the pattern you'll use constantly. Suppose the chance of rain tomorrow is 0.7. What's the chance of no rain?

"Rain" and "no rain" can't both happen, and together they cover everything (Rule 2 says everything totals 1). So:

chance of no rain  =  1 − 0.7  =  0.3

This "1 minus the chance" move has a name — the complement rule — and we didn't have to memorize it. It fell straight out of Rules 2 and 3. That's the whole spirit of this course.
