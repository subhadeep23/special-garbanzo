What a limit really is

A limit asks where the function is heading. It does not ask where the function arrives.

Why this lesson. Every limit sum in the paper starts with one decision, and she cannot make that decision until she separates two questions that look identical: what is the value at the point, and what is the value near the point. Get this apart today, and Lesson 2 takes ten minutes. Skip it, and every later lesson leaks.

Say it simply

You cook a dish. You taste it after every pinch of salt. The limit is the flavour the dish is heading towards. The value is the flavour of the finished dish on the plate.

Usually they agree. Sometimes the dish burns at the last second, and the plate tastes nothing like the taste it was heading to. A limit describes the tasting, never the plate.

Her own picture — use these words all year

She built this frame herself, so it is the frame this whole pack uses. Every later lesson points back to this table.

Maths wordIn the kitchen
Function  fThe recipe — the process that turns ingredients into a dish
Input  xThe ingredient she puts in
Output  f(x)The dish that comes out
DomainEvery ingredient the recipe accepts. A stone is not one of them
RangeEvery dish the recipe can produce
Value  f(a)The taste of the dish when she uses exactly that amount
Limit as x → aThe taste the dish heads to as the amount gets nearer and nearer to a

One warning, and it matters. Cooking happens in time. A limit does not. So never say "the taste before the dish is ready". Always name the ingredient: "the taste as the salt goes to 2 grams". The thing that moves is the ingredient, not the clock. Lesson 6 needs this, because "approach from above" has no meaning in a story about time.

Full word list, and the other three places the picture breaks: the kitchen dictionary.

The two questions

Take this function.

f(x) = x2 − 9x − 3

Question 1 — the value. What is f(3)?

f(3) = 9 − 93 − 3 = 00  —  not defined

Division by zero has no meaning. So f(3) does not exist. The function has a hole at x = 3. That is the honest answer, and it is the end of question 1.

Question 2 — the limit. Where is f(x) heading as x gets close to 3? Now we are not allowed to touch 3. We may only come near it. So put in numbers near 3 and watch.

from the leftf(x)from the rightf(x)
x = 2.95.9x = 3.16.1
x = 2.995.99x = 3.016.01
x = 2.9995.999x = 3.0016.001
x = 2.99995.9999x = 3.00016.0001
x = 3no value — the hole

Both columns march towards 6. They never reach 6, and they do not need to. The direction of travel is unmistakable. So we write:

limx → 3 x2 − 9x − 3 = 6

Read the notation aloud, every time

limx → 3 means "as x approaches 3". The arrow is a journey, not an equals sign. x never becomes 3. x only gets near 3.

The sentence she must be able to say

f(3) does not exist, but limx→3f(x) = 6. These two statements do not contradict each other. They answer different questions.

Why a hole cannot stop a limit

Here is the cleanest proof that the limit ignores the point itself. Take a function built to be strange.

h(x) = 5 for every x ≠ 1,    and    h(1) = 100

Walk towards x = 1 from either side. Every single value you pass is 5. At 0.9 it is 5. At 0.99999 it is 5. At 1.00001 it is 5. So the function is heading to 5, beyond any doubt.

limx → 1h(x) = 5     while     h(1) = 100

The value at the point is 100 and the limit is 5. The limit never looks at the point. It looks only at the neighbourhood. This one example settles the whole idea.

Two sides, and how a limit fails

She approached from the left and from the right. Those two walks have names, and ISC uses the names.

NameShortNotationMeaning
Left-hand limitLHL limx → a−f(x) Walk towards a using values smaller than a
Right-hand limitRHL limx → a+f(x) Walk towards a using values larger than a
The existence rule — learn it in this exact form

The limit exists only if LHL = RHL, and that shared answer is a finite number.

limx→af(x) = L   ⇔   limx→a−f(x) = limx→a+f(x) = L

Two cooks make the same dish. One salts it pinch by pinch and creeps up on 2 grams from below. The other over-salts it and dilutes down to 2 grams from above. If both taste the same flavour, that flavour is the limit. If they taste two different flavours, there is no single flavour to name, so the limit does not exist.

So a limit fails in exactly three ways. Name them, because naming them stops her from writing a number where "does not exist" belongs.

1

The two sides disagree — a jump

f(x) = |x|x   at  x → 0

For any negative x, |x| = −x, so the fraction is −1. So LHL = −1. For any positive x, |x| = x, so the fraction is +1. So RHL = +1.

LHL ≠ RHL, therefore the limit does not exist. Notice that both one-sided limits exist perfectly well. They simply disagree. Lesson 6 lives here.

2

It grows without bound

f(x) = 1x2   at  x → 0

At 0.1 it is 100. At 0.01 it is 10 000. At 0.001 it is 1 000 000. The dish does not head to a flavour. It gets more and more salty without any end, so no flavour can be named. We write ∞ to describe that behaviour, and we still say the limit does not exist, because ∞ is not a number.

3

It oscillates forever

f(x) = sin1x   at  x → 0

The values swing between −1 and +1 for ever, faster and faster. There is no direction of travel at all. The limit does not exist.

ISC will not ask her to compute this. Show it once, for 30 seconds, so that "does not exist" becomes a real answer in her mind and not a way of giving up.

The mistake this lesson prevents

She writes limx→3 x2−9x−3 = 00 and stops, or she writes "not defined".

The real gap: she is answering question 1 when the paper asked question 2. She substituted and treated the result as the final answer. The fix: 00 is never an answer. It is a signal that the work has not started. Lesson 2 is entirely about reading that signal.

Exercise set

Attempt each sum before she opens a hint. She writes the hint level she needed: H0 means alone, then H1, H2, H3, H4.

  1. Easy Explain in two sentences, in her own words, why limx→2f(x) can exist even when f(2) does not.
    Hint 1 — the type This is a definition question, not a calculation. No working is needed.
    Hint 2 — the move Use the kitchen picture. A limit describes the tasting or the plate. Which one?
    Answer A limit describes the values of the function at points near 2, never at 2 itself. So a hole exactly at x = 2 removes the value but leaves the direction of travel untouched. In her words: the tasting still tells her where the flavour was heading, even when the dish never reaches the plate.
  2. Easy Build a table for x2−25x−5 at x = 4.9, 4.99, 4.999, 5.001, 5.01, 5.1. State the limit as x → 5.
    Hint 1 — the type A numerical table, exactly like the one in this lesson. Compute, do not factorise.
    Hint 2 — the move For x = 4.9 the value is 9.9. Now see the pattern before computing the rest.
    Answer The values are 9.9, 9.99, 9.999, 10.001, 10.01, 10.1. Both sides head to 10. (The function equals x + 5 everywhere except at x = 5.)
  3. Medium For f(x) = |x − 2|x − 2, find the LHL and the RHL at x = 2. Does the limit exist?
    Hint 1 — the type A modulus function, so the two sides must be checked separately. Never substitute.
    Hint 2 — the move For x < 2, the quantity x − 2 is negative, so |x − 2| = −(x − 2).
    Hint 3 — the first line LHL = limx→2− −(x−2)x−2 = limx→2−(−1)
    Answer LHL = −1 and RHL = +1. They disagree, so the limit does not exist. Both one-sided limits do exist. Say that in the answer — it earns the mark.
  4. Medium Sketch any function that satisfies all three at once: f(1) = 4, LHL at 1 = 2, RHL at 1 = 2.
    Hint 1 — the type A construction question. She must invent it. There is more than one right answer.
    Hint 2 — the move The limit must be 2, and the value must be 4. So the graph approaches height 2 from both sides, and the single point at x = 1 sits somewhere else.
    Answer Draw the horizontal line y = 2 with an open circle at (1, 2), and a filled dot at (1, 4). Written as a rule: f(x) = 2 for x ≠ 1, and f(1) = 4. The limit is 2 and the value is 4.
  5. Hard A student writes: "limx→0 1x = ∞, so the limit exists and equals infinity." Find the two separate errors.
    Hint 1 — the type An error-hunting question. One error is about the two sides. One is about what a number is.
    Hint 2 — the move Test x = −0.001, then test x = +0.001. Compare the signs.
    Answer Error 1. The two sides disagree. From the left the values fall to −∞; from the right they rise to +∞. So even the unbounded behaviour is not the same on both sides.
    Error 2. ∞ is not a number, so no limit can "equal" it. A limit exists only when it equals a finite number.
    Correct answer: the limit does not exist.

    Note the contrast with 1/x2, where both sides rise to +∞. That limit also does not exist, but for error 2 alone.
  6. Stretch Is it possible for LHL and RHL to both exist, be equal, and the limit still fail to exist? Justify the answer.
    Hint 1 — the type A logic question about the existence rule. Read the rule again, word by word.
    Answer No. The existence rule is an "if and only if": when LHL = RHL = L and L is finite, the limit exists and equals L. Nothing else is required. In particular the value f(a) is irrelevant, and it may be anything or may not exist at all.

    The one thing to watch: "equal" must mean equal to a finite number. If both sides rise to +∞, they are not equal to a number, and the limit does not exist.
Close the lesson — she says this, not you

"A limit is where the function is heading. The value is where it arrives. The limit exists only when both sides head to the same finite number, and it does not care what happens at the point itself."

Her kitchen version, which is worth just as many marks in her own head: "The limit is the taste the dish is heading to. The value is the taste on the plate. Both cooks must taste the same flavour, or there is no limit."
