Exponential and logarithmic limits

Three more recipes she knows by heart. The technique is the one she already has from Lesson 4.

Why this lesson. The board names exponential and logarithmic limits in the topic text for Unit 4.1 They cost 30 minutes, because the method is not new — it is Lesson 4 again, with three new standard forms. Teach nothing new here except the three forms. Everything else is revision, and she should be told that at the start, so she does not brace for a hard lesson.

Say it simply

These three results are given. She does not derive them. She learns to recognise them and to force the match, exactly as she did with sin x/x.

The same idea in her kitchen

Lesson 4 gave her one recipe she knows by heart. This lesson gives her three more. The cooking method does not change. Only the number of memorised recipes changes.

Every one of them is written for a pinch — the ingredient must go to zero. Every one of them wants the same ingredient on the top and on the bottom. When they do not match, she scales the measuring cup until they do, exactly as before.

Why this lesson is short: she is not learning to cook a new dish. She is adding three cards to a recipe box she already knows how to use. Say that to her in the first minute of the session.

The three standard forms

Learn these three exactly

limx→0 ex − 1x = 1

limx→0 ax − 1x = loge a   (for a > 0)

limx→0 loge(1 + x)x = 1

The first is a special case of the second, because loge e = 1. Show her that once. Two of the three then collapse into one thing to remember.

The shape they share — this is the whole lesson

Look at all three. Each one is

(something that becomes 0)(the same something)  →  a fixed number

That is the identical shape as sin x/x. So the identical technique works: check the ingredient goes to zero, then force the match. She has no new skill to learn today.

Notation — settle this before any sum

In this unit log x always means loge x, the natural logarithm. Some books write it ln x. NCERT and ISC write log x and mean base e. If a question ever means base 10, it says log10 explicitly.

She must also know log 1 = 0. That single fact is why the third form is a 0/0 and not something else. Make her check it: at x = 0 the top is log(1 + 0) = log 1 = 0, and the bottom is 0. So it is 0/0, and a standard form is allowed.

Force the match — three worked examples

Worked example 1 — the coefficient does not match

limx→0 e5x − 1x

Step 1 — substitute first, as always. Top: e0 − 1 = 0. Bottom: 0. So it is 0/0, and a standard form is allowed.

Step 2 — the recipe wants 5x on the bottom. Put it there, and multiply by 5 outside.

= limx→0 e5x − 15x × 5 = 1 × 5 = 5

Step 3 — say why the standard form applied: as x → 0, the quantity 5x → 0 too. Both conditions hold.

Worked example 2 — base a, not base e

limx→0 32x − 1x

Step 1 — substitute: 30 − 1 = 0 over 0. It is 0/0.

Step 2 — force the match to 2x.

= limx→0 32x − 12x × 2 = (loge 3) × 2 = 2 loge 3

She may also write the answer as loge 9, because 2 log 3 = log 32. Both earn the mark. Encourage the second form only if she is fluent with log laws, and never at the cost of a slip.

Worked example 3 — a ratio of two standard forms

limx→0 e4x − 1loge(1 + 3x)

Step 1 — substitute: 0 on top, log 1 = 0 on the bottom. So 0/0.

Step 2 — neither part matches. Divide the top and the bottom by x, so that each becomes a standard form on its own.

= limx→0 (e4x − 1) ÷ x loge(1 + 3x) ÷ x = 43 = 43

The top forces to 4x and gives 4. The bottom forces to 3x and gives 3. Then the quotient law finishes it, because the bottom limit 3 is not zero. Two standard forms in one sum is the normal ISC question shape.

The two limits that define e

Two more results appear in textbooks. She should recognise them and not fear them. ISC Class 11 rarely asks them directly.

limx→0(1 + x)1/x = e      limx→∞ (1 + 1x)x = e

These two are the definition of the number e, not a result to derive. e = 2.71828… Tell her that, show her the two forms once, and move on. Spending a session here earns nothing.

Limits at infinity — the ∞∞ form

Lesson 2 promised this form here. It appears when x → ∞ in a rational function. There is one move, and it always works.

The move — divide by the highest power in the denominator

Then use the single fact that carries every one of these sums:

limx→∞ 1xn = 0    for every n > 0

Worked example 4

limx→∞ 3x2 + 5x − 17x2 − 2

Step 1 — the highest power on the bottom is x2. Divide every term, top and bottom, by x2.

= limx→∞ 3 + 5/x − 1/x2 7 − 2/x2 = 3 + 0 − 07 − 0 = 37

The shortcut, once she has done five by hand

Compare the highest power on the top with the highest power on the bottom.

CaseAnswerWhy
Top power < bottom power0The bottom grows faster, so the fraction shrinks
Top power = bottom powerRatio of the leading coefficientsThey grow at the same rate
Top power > bottom powerDoes not existIt grows without bound. Say → ∞ as a description

She must still show the dividing step in the answer. The shortcut is for checking her answer, never for replacing the working. ISC marks the method.

The three mistakes this lesson prevents

1. She writes limx→0 e5x−1x = 1, because "that is the formula".
The real gap: she matched the shape and not the ingredient. The formula needs the same quantity on the top and the bottom. This is the identical error as sin 3x/x = 1 from Lesson 4, so name it as the same error, not a new one.

2. She writes limx→0 log xx = 1.
The real gap: the standard form is log(1 + x), not log x. At x = 0, log x is not even defined — 0 is outside the domain of the log. In her words: the recipe rejects that ingredient.

3. She treats ∞ as a number and writes ∞/∞ = 1.
The real gap: the same gap as 0/0 = 1 from Lesson 2. It is an indeterminate form, so it carries no information. She divides by the highest power instead.

Exercise set

  1. Easy Evaluate limx→0 e7x − 1x.
    Hint 1 — the type A single standard form with a coefficient that does not match.
    Hint 2 — the move Put 7x on the bottom and multiply by 7 outside.
    Answer = limx→0 e7x−17x × 7 = 1 × 7 = 7.
  2. Easy Evaluate limx→0 log(1 + 5x)2x.
    Hint 1 — the type A log standard form, with a constant on the bottom as well as a mismatch.
    Hint 2 — the move Handle the two separately: take 1/2 outside as a constant, then force the match to 5x.
    Answer = 12 × limx→0 log(1+5x)5x × 5 = 12 × 1 × 5 = 5/2.
  3. Medium Evaluate limx→0 4x − 3xx.
    Hint 1 — the type Two exponentials in a difference. Neither one is a standard form on its own, because neither has a − 1.
    Hint 2 — the move Add and subtract 1 on the top. That splits it into two standard forms.
    Hint 3 — the first line 4x − 3x = (4x − 1) − (3x − 1)
    Answer Split it: = limx→0 4x−1x − limx→0 3x−1x = log 4 − log 3 = log(4/3).
    Learn the move, not the answer. "Add and subtract 1" is the standard trick for every difference of two exponentials, and ISC likes it.
  4. Medium Evaluate limx→∞ (2x − 1)2(3x + 4)6x3 + x − 5.
    Hint 1 — the type A limit at infinity. Find the highest power on the top without expanding everything.
    Hint 2 — the move The top has degree 2 + 1 = 3. The bottom has degree 3. They are equal, so use the leading coefficients only.
    Answer Leading term on the top: (2x)2(3x) = 12x3. Leading term on the bottom: 6x3. Equal degrees, so the limit is 12/6 = 2.
    In the written answer, divide the top and the bottom by x3 and show that every remaining term with an x underneath goes to 0.
  5. Hard Evaluate limx→0 e3x − e2xsin 4x.
    Hint 1 — the type A difference of exponentials on the top, a Lesson 4 form on the bottom. Substitution gives 0/0.
    Hint 2 — the move Two choices for the top. Either add and subtract 1, or take e2x out as a common factor. Then divide the whole thing by x.
    Hint 3 — the first line Divide the top and the bottom by x, then write the top as e3x−1x − e2x−1x.
    Answer Top ÷ x → 3 − 2 = 1. Bottom ÷ x: sin 4x/x → 4. So the answer is 1/4.
    The quotient law applies because the bottom limit 4 is not zero. Say that in the answer — it is a mark.
  6. Stretch A student says: "log(1 + x)/x → 1, so for very small x we may write log(1 + x) ≈ x." Is that a legitimate statement? Use it to predict log(1.002), then check the prediction on a calculator.
    Hint 1 — the type A question about what a limit actually claims. It is a reasoning question, and the calculator is the evidence.
    Hint 2 — the move Write 1.002 as 1 + 0.002. So the prediction is log(1.002) ≈ 0.002.
    Answer Yes, it is legitimate as an approximation for small x, and no, it is not an equation. The limit says the ratio heads to 1, so the two quantities become close relative to each other as x shrinks. It never says they are equal.
    Prediction: 0.002. Calculator: loge(1.002) = 0.0019980…
    The prediction is right to five decimal places at x = 0.002, and it gets worse as x grows: at x = 1, log 2 = 0.693, not 1.

    Why this matters. Every standard limit in this pack is exactly this kind of statement — a claim about behaviour near a point, never an identity. The same reading applies to sin x ≈ x from Lesson 4.
Close the lesson — she says this, not you

"There are three more standard forms: ex−1 over x goes to 1, ax−1 over x goes to log a, and log(1+x) over x goes to 1. I use them the same way as sin x/x: substitute first, check the ingredient goes to zero, then force the match."

Her kitchen version: "Three more cards for the recipe box. Same box, same method. Each one is written for a pinch, and each one wants the same ingredient top and bottom."

If anything here is unclear

Ask. The one question worth asking early: "why is the answer log a and not 1?" The short answer is that e is the one base where the growth rate at the start is exactly 1, and every other base is that rate scaled by log a. She does not need to prove that. She needs to stop writing 1 for base 2.
