The rules of differentiation

Five rules replace the four steps. Two of them are not what a student guesses, and those two are where the marks are.

Why this lesson. First principles is correct but slow. The syllabus names the derivative of a sum, a difference, a product, and a quotient.1 The sum and difference rules are what she would guess. The product and quotient rules are not. Teach the two surprising ones hard, and prove to her once that the guess is wrong, or she will keep using it under time pressure.

Say it simply

Adding is easy: differentiate each piece and add. Multiplying is not. When two things change at the same time, the total change has two sources, and she must count both.

The same idea in her kitchen

Two recipes, cooked together. Adding two dishes to the same plate is easy. Multiplying is where the surprise is.

The sum rule. Rice on one side of the plate, dal on the other. Change the ingredient, and each side changes on its own. The two do not interfere. So she works out each change and adds them.

The product rule. Now think of the total salt in a pot:

total salt = (salt per litre) × (litres in the pot)

Suppose both are changing. She adds a little salt, and at the same time more liquid boils in. How much did the total salt move? Two things caused it:

  • the concentration changed, over the volume she already had, and
  • the volume changed, at the concentration she already had.

Add those two contributions, and that is the product rule. Written out:

(uv)′ = u′v + uv′

This is why u′v′ is wrong. That answer counts neither contribution properly. It multiplies two rates together, and a rate times a rate is not a rate. In the kitchen it is the claim that nothing changes unless both change at once, which is plainly false — pouring in salt changes the total even when the volume sits still.

The three easy rules

These three behave exactly as she expects
RuleStatementExample
Power ddx(xn) = n xn−1 x6 → 6x5
Constant multiple (k f)′ = k f′ 5x3 → 15x2
Sum and difference (f ± g)′ = f′ ± g′ x2 − sin x → 2x − cos x

The power rule works for every n — positive, negative, or fractional. That single fact removes three separate results she would otherwise memorise.

1x = x−1  →  −1x−2 = −1x2    ·    √x = x1/2  →  12x−1/2 = 12√x

Both agree with the first-principles answers from Lesson 7. Make her check that herself. It is the best evidence that the rules are shortcuts and not new magic.

The habit that saves the most time

Rewrite before differentiating. Turn every root and every fraction into a power first. Then the power rule alone finishes many sums that look as if they need the quotient rule.

x3 + 2xx = x2 + 2  →  2x

No quotient rule was needed, and the sum took four seconds. Look for this every time she sees a fraction. ISC sets at least one question a year that is far easier after a rewrite.

The product rule

Learn it in this exact form

(uv)′ = u′v + uv′

Said aloud: "first differentiated times second, plus first times second differentiated." Make her say it while she writes it, for the first ten sums.

Prove the guess wrong once, with numbers

Take f(x) = x × x. Then f(x) = x2, so the true derivative is 2x.

The guess u′v′ gives 1 × 1 = 1.

The product rule gives (1)(x) + (x)(1) = 2x. Correct.

Do this on the board before teaching the rule, not after. She then learns the rule as the answer to a problem she has already seen, and not as an arbitrary formula.

Worked example 1 — y = x2 sin x

Step 1 — label the two parts. Write them down. Do not hold them in her head.

u = x2,   u′ = 2x    ·    v = sin x,   v′ = cos x

Step 2 — put them into the rule, in order.

dydx = (2x)(sin x) + (x2)(cos x) = 2x sin x + x2 cos x

Do not expand or "tidy" this. It is a finished answer. She may factorise to x(2 sin x + x cos x) if she wishes, and both forms earn the mark.

The quotient rule

Learn it in this exact form

(uv)′ = u′v − uv′ v2

Said aloud: "top differentiated times bottom, minus top times bottom differentiated, all over bottom squared."

The order is the whole rule

The product rule has a plus, so the order of the two terms does not matter. The quotient rule has a minus, so the order decides the sign of the whole answer. Writing uv′ − u′v gives the correct answer with the wrong sign, every single time.

The fix that works: the term that starts with the differentiated top comes first. She says "top-dash-bottom, minus top-bottom-dash" out loud as she writes. Three seconds, and it removes the most common error in the whole unit.

Worked example 2 — y = sin x / x

Step 1 — label. The top is u, the bottom is v.

u = sin x,   u′ = cos x    ·    v = x,   v′ = 1

Step 2 — put them in, keeping the order.

dydx = (cos x)(x) − (sin x)(1) x2 = x cos x − sin x x2

Worked example 3 — tan x, and where sec2 x comes from

tan x = sin x / cos x, so the quotient rule applies.

u = sin x,   u′ = cos x    ·    v = cos x,   v′ = −sin x

= (cos x)(cos x) − (sin x)(−sin x) cos2 x = cos2 x + sin2 x cos2 x = 1 cos2 x = sec2 x

Watch the double minus. v′ = −sin x, and the rule already has a minus, so the two make a plus. That is what turns the top into the Pythagorean identity. Most errors in this sum are one missing minus sign, not a misunderstanding of the rule.

The four mistakes this lesson prevents

1. She writes (uv)′ = u′v′.
The real gap: she is carrying the sum rule across to products. The sum rule works because the two pieces do not interfere. In a product they do — changing one changes the effect of the other. Send her back to the x × x check.

2. She writes the quotient rule with the terms reversed.
The real gap: she memorised a shape and not an order. The minus sign is not decoration. Make her say "top-dash-bottom first" aloud.

3. She uses the quotient rule on (x3 + 2x)/x.
The real gap: she matches the tool to the appearance instead of to the sum. Divide the terms out first, and the power rule finishes it. Always look for the rewrite before choosing a rule.

4. She writes d/dx(x−3) = −3x−2.
The real gap: she moved the power towards zero instead of subtracting 1. Subtracting 1 from −3 gives −4, not −2. The rule is the same for negative powers, and the arithmetic is where it breaks.

Exercise set

  1. Easy Differentiate y = 5x4 − 3x2 + 2x − 8.
    Hint 1 — the type Sum rule and power rule, term by term. No product or quotient anywhere.
    Answer dy/dx = 20x3 − 6x + 2. The constant −8 gives 0 and vanishes.
  2. Easy Differentiate y = x3 cos x.
    Hint 1 — the type A product of two different kinds of function, so the product rule is needed.
    Hint 2 — the move Label u = x3 and v = cos x, and write u′ and v′ before touching the rule.
    Answer u′ = 3x2, v′ = −sin x.
    dy/dx = 3x2 cos x − x3 sin x.
    The minus sign came from v′, not from the rule. The product rule itself has a plus.
  3. Medium Differentiate y = x2 + 1x − 1.
    Hint 1 — the type A genuine quotient — the top does not divide cleanly by the bottom, so no rewrite helps.
    Hint 2 — the move u = x2+1 and v = x−1. Say "top-dash-bottom first" as she writes.
    Hint 3 — the first line dy/dx = [(2x)(x−1) − (x2+1)(1)] / (x−1)2
    Answer Top = 2x2 − 2x − x2 − 1 = x2 − 2x − 1.
    dy/dx = (x2 − 2x − 1) / (x−1)2.
    Leave the bottom as (x−1)2. Expanding it earns nothing and risks a slip.
  4. Medium Find the slope of the tangent to y = x sin x at x = π/2.
    Hint 1 — the type Product rule, then substitute. Two steps, in that order.
    Hint 2 — the move Recall sin(π/2) = 1 and cos(π/2) = 0. Differentiate first, substitute second.
    Answer dy/dx = sin x + x cos x.
    At x = π/2: 1 + (π/2)(0) = 1.
    The second term vanished because the cosine is zero there. Watch for that — students often compute (π/2)(0) as π/2.
  5. Hard Differentiate y = x2 sin x cos x.
    Hint 1 — the type Three factors, and she has a rule for two. There are two good routes.
    Hint 2 — the move Either group it as u = x2 and v = sin x cos x, applying the product rule twice, or use the identity sin x cos x = ½ sin 2x first.
    Hint 3 — the first line Grouping route: first find (sin x cos x)′ = cos2 x − sin2 x.
    Answer Inner product: (sin x cos x)′ = (cos x)(cos x) + (sin x)(−sin x) = cos2 x − sin2 x.
    Then the outer product rule:
    dy/dx = 2x sin x cos x + x2(cos2 x − sin2 x).

    The identity route. y = ½ x2 sin 2x. This is neater, but differentiating sin 2x needs the chain rule, which is Class 12. So at Class 11 the grouping route is the one she must use. Recognising that a shortcut needs a tool she does not have yet is itself a skill.
  6. Stretch Derive the quotient rule from the product rule. Start by writing u = (u/v) × v.
    Hint 1 — the type A derivation, not a calculation. It shows the two rules are one rule.
    Hint 2 — the move Call q = u/v. Then u = qv. Differentiate both sides with the product rule, then make q′ the subject.
    Hint 3 — the first line u′ = q′v + qv′
    Answer From u′ = q′v + qv′, make q′ the subject:
    q′v = u′ − qv′ = u′ − (u/v)v′.
    Divide by v: q′ = u′/v − uv′/v2.
    Put it over one denominator: q′ = (u′v − uv′)/v2. That is the quotient rule.

    Why this is worth twenty minutes. The minus sign is no longer something to remember — it appears from the algebra, in front of her. A student who has done this once stops reversing the order. It is also a fair ISC question, because the syllabus names both rules.
Close the lesson — she says this, not you

"Sums differentiate term by term. Products do not: I use u′v + uv′, because two things changing at once give two contributions. Quotients use (u′v − uv′)/v2, and the order matters because of the minus. Before I choose a rule, I check whether a rewrite makes it a power-rule sum."

Her kitchen version: "Two dishes on one plate just add. But the total salt in a pot depends on the concentration and the volume together, so when both move I count both contributions."

If anything here is unclear

Ask. The one question worth asking early: "how do I know which rule to use?" The order is fixed. First ask whether a rewrite turns it into powers. If not, ask whether the parts are added, multiplied, or divided. The structure of the expression chooses the rule — she never has to guess.
