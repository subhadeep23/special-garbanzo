Trigonometric limits

One standard result carries almost every sum. It has two conditions, and she must check both.

Why this lesson. ISC asks a trigonometric limit almost every year. There is essentially one formula, and the marks are lost not on the formula but on its two conditions. Teach the conditions harder than the formula.

Say it simply

For a very small angle, sin x and x are almost the same number. So their ratio heads to 1.

Check it on a calculator, in radian mode. sin(0.01) = 0.00999983… That is 0.01 to four decimal places. The smaller the angle, the closer they get. That is the entire content of the formula, and she should see it once with her own hands.

The same idea in her kitchen

A standard limit is a recipe she already knows by heart. She may use it without any working. But a known recipe only works when she gives it the ingredient it asks for.

The recipe reads: sin(◻) ÷ (◻) → 1, and the two boxes must hold the same ingredient. If the recipe asks for 3x and she has x on the bottom, it is not that recipe. It is a different dish, and it gives a different taste.

"Force the match" in her words

The recipe needs 3x on the bottom, and she has x. So she writes 3x on the bottom and multiplies by 3 outside. She scales the measuring cup, not the dish. The dish is unchanged, so the move is always legal.

Radians are the unit the recipe is written in. Degrees are a different unit, and a recipe measured in cups gives the wrong dish when she reads the numbers as spoons. In degrees the answer becomes π/180, not 1.

The three standard results

Learn these three exactly

limx→0 sin xx = 1

limx→0 1 − cos xx = 0

limx→0 tan xx = 1

The first two are stated as the two important limits in NCERT.2 The third follows at once, because tan x = sin x / cos x, and cos x → 1.

And one more, which the paper likes because students guess it wrongly:

limx→0 1 − cos xx2 = 12

Note carefully that (1 − cos x)/x → 0 while (1 − cos x)/x2 → 1/2. One extra power of x changes 0 into 1/2. She must read the denominator, not recognise the numerator. Section 4 below derives the 1/2.

The two conditions

Here is where the marks go. The formula sin x/x → 1 needs both of these to be true.

1

The angle must go to zero

Not x. The angle. If the angle does not head to 0, the formula does not apply, and usually plain substitution finishes the sum instead.

limx→π/2 sin xx = sin(π/2)π/2 = 1π/2 = 2π

Here x → π/2, so the angle does not go to zero. The form is 1/(π/2), which is a perfectly good number. So there is nothing indeterminate, and no formula is needed. Substituting first, as in Lesson 2, catches this automatically.

2

The angle must match the denominator, exactly

The formula is sin(◻) / (◻) → 1, where the two boxes hold the same thing. sin 3x over x is not the formula. sin 3x over 3x is.

The technique — force the match

Multiply and divide by whatever makes the bottom match the angle. Multiplying and dividing by the same non-zero number changes nothing, so the move is always legal.

limx→0 sin 3xx = limx→0 sin 3x3x × 3 = 1 × 3 = 3

Read the middle step aloud: "I need 3x on the bottom, so I put 3x there and multiply by 3 outside to keep the value the same."

The pattern, once she trusts it:

limx→0 sin kxx = k    ·    limx→0 sin axsin bx = ab    ·    limx→0 tan axsin bx = ab

Do not let her memorise these three. Make her produce each one by forcing the match. The memorised version fails the moment the paper writes sin x2/x2 or sin(x/4)/x. The technique never fails.

Radians, not degrees. This is not a detail

The result sin x/x → 1 is only true in radians. In degrees the answer is π/180, which is about 0.01745.

Every angle in calculus is in radians. If her calculator says sin(0.01) = 0.0001745, the calculator is in degree mode. Make her fix the mode before she checks anything, or she will disbelieve the correct formula.

Where the formula comes from

ISC asks for the intuitive idea, so this is one board and 90 seconds. Do it once, because it explains why radians are compulsory, and that stops the degree mistake for good.

For a small positive angle x in radians, comparing areas or arc lengths in the unit circle gives:

cos x  <  sin xx  <  1

As x → 0, the left side cos x → 1 and the right side is already 1. So sin x/x is squeezed between two quantities that both head to 1. It has nowhere else to go.

The Sandwich Theorem — state it, do not prove it

If f(x) ≤ g(x) ≤ h(x) for all x near a, and limx→af(x) = limx→ah(x) = L, then limx→ag(x) = L.2

Two walls close in on the middle function, so the middle function must arrive where the walls arrive. That is the whole theorem, and the syllabus asks for the statement only.

The unit-circle inequality also shows why the angle must be in radians. The arc length of a unit circle equals the angle only when the angle is measured in radians. That is the whole reason.

The 1 − cos x family

Everything here comes from one identity, taken from Unit 1(iii) Trigonometry:

1 − cos x = 2 sin2 x2

Check she knows this cold before continuing. Without it, nothing in this section works.

Worked example — the 1/2 result

limx→0 1 − cos xx2

Step 1 — substitute: (1−1)/0 = 0/0. Work needed.

Step 2 — replace the top using the identity.

= limx→0 2 sin2(x/2)x2

Step 3 — the angle is x/2, so force the denominator to match it. Write x2 = 4(x/2)2.

= limx→0 2 sin2(x/2)4 (x/2)2 = 12 limx→0 [sin(x/2)x/2]2

Step 4 — the bracket → 1, so its square → 1.

= 12 × 1 = 12

The general version, by the same route:

limx→0 1 − cos kxx2 = k22

When the angle does not go to zero: substitute

The paper will sometimes send x to π or to a, and still expect the standard formula. The move is a change of variable, so that the new variable goes to zero.

Worked example — x → π

limx→π sin xπ − x

Step 1 — substitute: sin π = 0 and π − π = 0. So 0/0.

Step 2 — put h = π − x. Then x = π − h, and as x → π the new variable h → 0.

Step 3 — sin(π − h) = sin h, from Unit 1(iii).

= limh→0 sin hh = 1

The substitution h = x − a, or h = a − x, turns any approach point into zero. It is the same trick that first principles uses in Lesson 7, so it is worth the practice now.

Exercise set

  1. Easy Evaluate limx→0 sin 7x2x.
    Hint 1 — the typeMatching, with a constant on the bottom.
    Answer = (1/2)(sin 7x/7x)(7) → (1/2)(1)(7) = 7/2.
  2. Easy Evaluate limx→0 x + sin xx.
    Hint 1 — the typeSplit it into two fractions before doing anything else.
    Answer = lim[x/x + sin x/x] = 1 + 1 = 2. Splitting is legal by the sum law.
  3. Medium Evaluate limx→0 1 − cos 4x1 − cos 6x.
    Hint 1 — the typeTwo members of the 1 − cos family, one over the other.
    Hint 2 — the moveDivide the top and the bottom by x2, and use the k2/2 result on each.
    Answer Top/x2 → 42/2 = 8. Bottom/x2 → 62/2 = 18. So the answer is 8/18 = 4/9.
    By identity instead: 2sin22x / 2sin23x = (sin 2x/sin 3x)2 → (2/3)2 = 4/9. Same answer.
  4. Medium Evaluate limx→0 tan x − sin xx3.
    Hint 1 — the typeTake out the common factor sin x from the top first.
    Hint 2 — the movetan x − sin x = sin x(1/cos x − 1) = sin x · (1 − cos x)/cos x.
    Hint 3 — the first lineNow the expression is (sin x/x) · ((1−cos x)/x2) · (1/cos x). Take the limit of each factor.
    Answer = (1) × (1/2) × (1) = 1/2.
    This sum is worth doing slowly. Notice the shape of the method: break the expression into a product of pieces, where every piece is a standard limit.
  5. Hard Evaluate limx→π/4 cos x − sin xx − π/4.
    Hint 1 — the typeThe angle does not go to zero, so change the variable.
    Hint 2 — the movePut h = x − π/4, so x = h + π/4 and h → 0. Expand cos and sin of the sum.
    Hint 3 — the first linecos(h+π/4) − sin(h+π/4) = (1/√2)[(cos h − sin h) − (sin h + cos h)] = (1/√2)(−2 sin h).
    Answer The expression becomes (−2/√2)(sin h/h) → −2/√2 = −√2.
    Sanity check: at x = π/4, cos x = sin x, so the top is 0 and the bottom is 0. Good, it was a genuine 0/0. Just past π/4, cosine is smaller than sine, so the top is negative and the bottom is positive. The answer must be negative, and it is.
  6. Stretch Evaluate limx→0 sin x − x cos xx3, using only the standard results in this lesson.
    Hint 1 — the typeSplit the fraction into pieces that are each standard. Do not expand anything.
    Hint 2 — the moveWrite the top as (sin x − x) + x(1 − cos x). Handle the second piece first, because it is standard.
    Answer Split:
    sin x − xx3 + x(1−cos x)x3
    The second piece is (1−cos x)/x2 → 1/2.
    The first piece is (sin x − x)/x3 → −1/6. This one is beyond the ISC Class 11 toolkit — it needs a series or a repeated derivative argument.
    So the answer is −1/6 + 1/2 = 1/3.

    The real lesson here. The correct response to this question in an exam is to recognise that it needs a tool she does not have, and to move on. Knowing the edge of her toolkit is a skill. This is the only sum in the pack that sits outside the syllabus, and it is here on purpose, so that she meets that edge with me and not in the exam hall.
Close the lesson — she says this, not you

"sin of something over that same something goes to 1, but only when the something goes to zero and the angle matches the bottom exactly. If they do not match, I multiply and divide to force the match. Everything is in radians."

Her kitchen version: "It is a recipe I know by heart, so I may use it without working. But it only works for a pinch, and it wants the same ingredient top and bottom. If they do not match, I scale the measuring cup until they do."

If anything here is unclear

Ask. The one question worth asking early: "how do I know whether to force the match or just substitute?" The answer is always Lesson 2 — substitute first. If it gives a number, stop. Only 0/0 sends her to the formula.
