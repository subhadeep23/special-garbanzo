One-sided limits

Three functions break the rule that substitution works. For those three, she must check both sides, every time.

Why this lesson. Lesson 2 taught her to substitute first. This lesson names the exact cases where substituting first is wrong. There are only three of them — modulus, greatest integer, and piecewise — and they are easy to spot. ISC asks them because they separate the students who understand a limit from the students who follow a procedure.

Say it simply

Some functions have a different rule on the left of a point and on the right of it. For those, one substitution cannot answer the question, because there is no single rule to substitute into. She must work the two sides separately, then compare.

The same idea in her kitchen

Two cooks make the same dish. One creeps up on 2 grams of salt from below, adding pinch by pinch. The other starts over-salted and dilutes down to 2 grams from above. If both taste the same flavour, that flavour is the limit. If they taste two different flavours, there is no single flavour to name, so the limit does not exist.

The three troublesome functions are recipes with a switch in them. The recipe card says: "below 2 grams, do it this way. Above 2 grams, do it that way." A cook coming from below follows one instruction. A cook coming from above follows a different one. That is why one taste cannot answer for both.

Maths wordIn the kitchen
LHL  limx→a− The cook who arrives from below the amount
RHL  limx→a+ The cook who arrives from above the amount
The limit existsBoth cooks report the same flavour
A jumpThe two cooks report different flavours. Neither is wrong. There is simply no single answer

Say "from below" and "from above", never "before" and "after". The two cooks are not separated in time. They are separated by which side of the amount they come from. Lesson 1 gave this warning, and this is the lesson where it earns its keep.

The rule, in the exact form ISC wants

The existence rule

limx→af(x) exists  ⇔  limx→a−f(x) = limx→a+f(x) = a finite number

Her written answer must show three lines: the LHL, the RHL, and the comparison. A correct final answer with no comparison line loses marks, because the comparison is the reasoning the question asks for.

The substitution that makes both sides easy

Working "just to the left of a" is hard to write. So replace x by a number that is definitely on the correct side, and let h shrink to zero.

LHL: put x = a − h      RHL: put x = a + h      where h → 0 and h > 0

The condition h > 0 is what does the work. It fixes the sign of every quantity in the sum, and the sign is the whole question. Make her write "h > 0" on the page, every time. It is one second and it prevents the commonest error in the lesson.

Case 1 — the modulus function

The modulus has a switch built into its definition. Write the definition every time before working:

|u| = u when u ≥ 0,     |u| = −u when u < 0

Read this twice

The switch is on the sign of what is inside the bars, not on the sign of x. For |x − 5|, the switch is at x = 5, not at x = 0. She must find where the inside becomes zero. That point is where the break is.

Worked example 1

limx→3 |x − 3|x − 3

Step 1 — the inside is zero at x = 3, and that is the point in question. So the two sides must be checked.

Step 2 — LHL. Put x = 3 − h with h > 0. Then x − 3 = −h, which is negative, so the bars flip the sign.

LHL = limh→0 |−h|−h = limh→0 h−h = −1

Step 3 — RHL. Put x = 3 + h with h > 0. Then x − 3 = h, which is positive, so the bars do nothing.

RHL = limh→0 hh = +1

Step 4 — compare. This line is compulsory.

LHL = −1 and RHL = +1. LHL ≠ RHL, so the limit does not exist. Note that both one-sided limits exist perfectly well. They simply disagree. Say that in the answer.

Case 2 — the greatest integer function

[x] means the largest integer that is not greater than x. It is also written ⌊x⌋ and is called the floor function or the step function.

x2.733.001−2.7−3−0.4
[x]233−3−3−1
The negative values are where she will lose the mark

[−2.7] is −3, not −2. The rule is "not greater than", and −3 < −2.7 < −2. So the largest integer that stays below is −3. It rounds down, never towards zero.

Make her fill this row from memory before the lesson goes on: [1.2], [−1.2], [4], [−4], [0.9], [−0.9]. Answers: 1, −2, 4, −4, 0, −1.

The one fact that solves every [x] limit

At an integer n, the graph jumps by exactly 1.

limx→n−[x] = n − 1      limx→n+[x] = n      [n] = n

So at every integer, the limit does not exist. Between integers, [x] is a constant, so the limit is that constant and there is nothing to check.

Worked example 2

Find limx→2[x] and limx→2.5[x].

First one. 2 is an integer, so check both sides.

Just below 2, the values of x are 1.9, 1.99, 1.999, and [x] = 1 for all of them. So LHL = 1.
Just above 2, the values are 2.001, 2.01, and [x] = 2. So RHL = 2.
LHL ≠ RHL, so the limit does not exist.

Second one. 2.5 is not an integer, so there is no break nearby.

Every x between 2 and 3 gives [x] = 2. So both sides give 2, and the limit is 2. In the kitchen: between the marks the recipe has no switch at all, so both cooks follow the same instruction.

Case 3 — piecewise functions

Here the switch is written out openly, which makes these the easiest of the three. The question always sits exactly at the switch point.

Worked example 3 — find the value of k

f(x) = 3x + 1  for x < 2,     f(x) = kx − 3  for x ≥ 2

Find k so that limx→2f(x) exists.

Step 1 — LHL uses the rule for x < 2, because the cook comes from below.

LHL = 3(2) + 1 = 7

Step 2 — RHL uses the rule for x ≥ 2.

RHL = k(2) − 3 = 2k − 3

Step 3 — for the limit to exist, set them equal.

2k − 3 = 7  ⇒  2k = 10  ⇒  k = 5

This shape — "find k so the limit exists" — is the most likely one-sided question in the paper. It is three lines of work and it is worth full marks. Drill it until it is automatic.

The four mistakes this lesson prevents

1. She substitutes straight into a modulus sum and gets 0/0, then factorises.
The real gap: she treats every 0/0 as a Lesson 3 sum. A modulus 0/0 has no shared factor to cancel. The bars are a switch, not an algebraic expression. The trigger to look for is the bars themselves, before any substitution.

2. She writes [−2.7] = −2.
The real gap: she is rounding towards zero, which is what a calculator's "integer part" button does. [x] rounds down, always. Fix it by drawing the number line and asking which integers are below −2.7.

3. She finds LHL = 2 and RHL = 6 and writes 4, or writes "the limit is 2 and 6".
The real gap: she believes every question has a numerical answer. "Does not exist" is the answer here, and it earns the full mark. Averaging earns zero.

4. She computes both sides correctly and writes the answer with no comparison line.
The real gap: she thinks the marks are for the numbers. The question asks whether the limit exists, so the comparison sentence is the answer. The two numbers are only the evidence.

Exercise set

  1. Easy Evaluate the LHL and the RHL of f(x) = |x − 2|x − 2 at x = 2, and state whether the limit exists.
    Hint 1 — the type A modulus over its own inside. Never substitute — write the definition of the modulus first.
    Hint 2 — the move Put x = 2 − h for the LHL and x = 2 + h for the RHL, with h > 0.
    Answer LHL: x − 2 = −h < 0, so the bars flip: h/(−h) = −1.
    RHL: x − 2 = h > 0, so h/h = +1.
    LHL ≠ RHL, so the limit does not exist. Both one-sided limits do exist.
  2. Easy Evaluate limx→4−[x], limx→4+[x], and [4].
    Hint 1 — the type The greatest integer function at an integer. Use the number line, not a formula.
    Answer LHL = 3 (values just below 4 give 3). RHL = 4. [4] = 4.
    So the limit does not exist at x = 4, and the value at the point equals the RHL. That is the shape of every jump in [x].
  3. Medium f(x) = x2 + 1 for x ≤ 1, and f(x) = 5x − 3 for x > 1. Does limx→1f(x) exist? What is f(1)?
    Hint 1 — the type A piecewise function at its switch point. Two separate substitutions, then compare.
    Hint 2 — the move The LHL uses the rule for x ≤ 1. The RHL uses the rule for x > 1. The value f(1) is a third, separate question.
    Answer LHL = 1 + 1 = 2. RHL = 5 − 3 = 2. They agree, so the limit exists and equals 2.
    f(1) uses the rule for x ≤ 1, so f(1) = 2 as well.
    Here the limit and the value happen to agree. Lesson 1 showed they need not. Make her say which of the three questions each line answered.
  4. Medium Find a and b so that limx→2f(x) exists and equals f(2), where f(x) = ax + 1 for x < 2, f(2) = 7, and f(x) = bx − 5 for x > 2.
    Hint 1 — the type Two unknowns, so she needs two equations. Each condition in the question gives one.
    Hint 2 — the move Condition A: LHL = 7. Condition B: RHL = 7. Solve them separately — they do not interact.
    Hint 3 — the first line LHL = 2a + 1, and this must equal 7.
    Answer 2a + 1 = 7 gives a = 3.
    2b − 5 = 7 gives b = 6.
    Check: LHL = 7, RHL = 7, f(2) = 7. All three agree.
    Note what she has just built. A function whose limit equals its value at a point is called continuous there. That word is Class 12 work, so do not define it formally — but she has now met the idea, and next year it will feel familiar rather than new.
  5. Hard Evaluate limx→2 x − 2|x − 2|  and  limx→0 |x|x + x. Explain why the two answers differ in kind.
    Hint 1 — the type Two modulus sums. The first is the earlier one turned upside down. The second adds a harmless term to it.
    Hint 2 — the move For the first, note that flipping a fraction upside down also flips −1 and +1 — which changes nothing, because 1/(−1) = −1.
    Answer First. LHL = (−h)/h = −1. RHL = h/h = +1. They disagree, so the limit does not exist. Turning the fraction over does not repair a jump.
    Second. LHL = −1 + 0 = −1. RHL = +1 + 0 = +1. Again the limit does not exist.
    Why they are the same in kind, not different. Adding x adds 0 to each side, so it moves neither one. A jump cannot be repaired by adding a well-behaved function to it. The gap of 2 between the two sides survives every such addition. That is the point of the question, and it is worth saying aloud.
  6. Stretch Evaluate limx→2([x] + [−x]). Then evaluate it at x → 2.5.
    Hint 1 — the type Two greatest integer functions added. Do not add the rules — build a small table of both sides.
    Hint 2 — the move Take x = 1.9. Then [x] = 1 and −x = −1.9, so [−x] = −2. Now take x = 2.1 and do the same.
    Answer At x → 2.
    From below (x = 1.9): 1 + (−2) = −1. So LHL = −1.
    From above (x = 2.1): 2 + (−3) = −1. So RHL = −1.
    They agree, so the limit is −1 — even though each part on its own jumps at 2. The two jumps cancel.
    Note that the value at the point is different: [2] + [−2] = 2 + (−2) = 0. So the limit is −1 and the value is 0. That is Lesson 1 again.

    At x → 2.5. Every x near 2.5 gives [x] = 2 and [−x] = −3, so the limit is −1 and the value is also −1.

    The idea worth taking away: a sum of two functions can have a limit even when neither part does. So she must never argue "this part has no limit, therefore the whole thing has no limit". She must check the whole expression.
Close the lesson — she says this, not you

"When I see a modulus, a greatest integer, or a piecewise rule, I do not substitute. I work the left-hand limit and the right-hand limit separately, using x = a − h and x = a + h with h > 0, and then I compare them. If they differ, the limit does not exist, and that is a full answer."

Her kitchen version: "Two cooks, one from below and one from above. If they taste the same flavour, that is the limit. If not, there is nothing to name."

If anything here is unclear

Ask. The one question worth asking early: "how do I know a sum needs two sides?" The answer is a list of three triggers, and nothing else: modulus bars, square brackets [x], or a rule written in pieces. If none of the three is present, Lesson 2 applies and she substitutes.
