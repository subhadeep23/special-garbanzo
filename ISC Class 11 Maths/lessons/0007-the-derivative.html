The derivative, from first principles

The derivative is one limit. It measures how sensitive the output is to a small change in the input.

Why this lesson. The syllabus names "differentiation using first principles" explicitly, so the board can ask for the full working and refuse a formula.1 This is also the lesson that pays next year: every one of the 35 Class 12 calculus marks stands on the idea built here. Give it the full 45 minutes even when time is short.

Say it simply

Change the input a little. See how much the output changes. Divide the change in the output by the change in the input. Then make the change in the input shrink to nothing. That number is the derivative.

The same idea in her kitchen

She adds one more pinch of salt and asks: how much did the taste move? Not what the taste is — how much it moved, per pinch. That is the derivative.

She already tastes while she cooks. That was the limit. The derivative is the next question she naturally asks in the same kitchen: how sensitive is this dish to salt?

SymbolIn the kitchen
hThe extra pinch she adds. A small amount, never zero
f(x + h) − f(x)How much the taste moved
f(x+h) − f(x)h Taste moved per pinch — the average sensitivity over that pinch
limh→0 of that The sensitivity for a pinch so small it is barely a pinch — the sensitivity right here

Two dishes, to make it concrete. A thin soup changes flavour a lot from one pinch, so its derivative is large. A heavy stew barely moves, so its derivative is small. Same pinch, different sensitivity. The derivative belongs to the recipe, not to the pinch.

And the sign. A positive derivative means more salt makes it saltier. A negative derivative means more of the ingredient moves the taste the other way — more water makes it blander. Zero means one more pinch changes nothing, which is exactly what happens at the top of a hill.

Why it must be a limit, and not a division

Ask her to compute the sensitivity at exactly one point, using no limit. She will write:

f(x) − f(x)x − x = 00

The whole reason calculus exists

Sensitivity at a single point is a 0/0. Lesson 2 named that form. Lesson 3 showed how to clear it. The derivative is the reason those two lessons were taught. She needed two points to measure a change, and the derivative asks for a change at one point, so the two points must slide together — and that sliding is a limit.

Say this to her once, plainly: "Everything we did with 0/0 was preparation for this." It is true, and it makes the earlier work feel paid for.

The two pictures ISC wants

1

Rate of change

If s(t) is the distance travelled at time t, then over a short interval h:

average speed = s(t+h) − s(t)h   →   speed right now, as h → 0

So the derivative of distance is speed. This is the board's own example — it names "rate of change ... of distance function" in the topic text.1 A speedometer reads a derivative.

2

Slope of the tangent

Take two points on the curve, at x and at x + h. The line through them is a chord, and its slope is

riserun = f(x+h) − f(x)(x+h) − x = f(x+h) − f(x)h

Now slide the second point towards the first. The chord turns, and it settles on the tangent — the line that just touches the curve at that one point. So:

the derivative at x = the slope of the tangent at x

Draw this once, by hand, with three chords getting shorter. Two minutes of drawing does more than a page of words. The syllabus asks for the geometrical interpretation, so this drawing is directly examinable.

Two pictures, one number

Rate of change and slope of the tangent are not two topics. They are two ways of reading the same limit. She should be able to say that sentence.

The definition, and the notation

First principles — learn this exactly

f′(x) = limh→0 f(x + h) − f(x)h

At one particular point x = a, there are two accepted forms. Both earn full marks, so she should learn the first and recognise the second.

f′(a) = limh→0 f(a + h) − f(a)h   =   limx→a f(x) − f(a)x − a

WrittenSaid aloudWhen to use it
f′(x)"f dash of x"When the function has a name
dydx"dy by dx"When the work is written as y = something
ddx[f(x)]"d by dx of ..."As an instruction: "differentiate this"
dy/dx is not a fraction

It is a single symbol that means "the derivative of y with respect to x". She must not cancel the ds. The notation is deliberately shaped like a fraction because it came from one, and in Class 12 it behaves like one in places — but at Class 11 it is one symbol. Cancelling the ds is the mark of a student who has not understood the definition.

The four steps — she writes all four, every time

The procedure
  1. Write f(x + h). Replace every x in the formula by (x + h). Use the brackets.
  2. Subtract f(x) and simplify the top. Everything without an h must cancel. If something without an h survives, there is an error above.
  3. Divide by h. Every surviving term has an h in it, so this always works.
  4. Let h → 0. Now substitute, because the 0/0 has gone.

Step 2 is the self-check. Teach her to stop and look: if a term with no h is left on the top, she has made a slip and she can find it herself, before she wastes the rest of the sum.

Worked example 1 — f(x) = x2

Step 1

f(x+h) = (x+h)2 = x2 + 2xh + h2

Step 2 — subtract. The x2 cancels, as it must.

f(x+h) − f(x) = 2xh + h2 = h(2x + h)

Step 3 — divide by h. This is legal because h is never zero, exactly as in Lesson 3.

h(2x+h)h = 2x + h

Step 4 — now let h → 0.

f′(x) = limh→0(2x + h) = 2x

Read the answer as sensitivity. At x = 1 the derivative is 2. At x = 5 it is 10. The same curve is five times more sensitive at 5 than at 1. In her kitchen: the same recipe reacts more sharply to one more pinch once there is already a lot of salt in it.

Worked example 2 — f(x) = 1/x, the LCM appears

Step 1 and 2 together. Take the LCM on the top — this is move 4 from Lesson 3.

1x+h − 1x = x − (x+h)x(x+h) = −hx(x+h)

Step 3 — divide by h.

= −1x(x+h)

Step 4 — let h → 0.

f′(x) = −1x2

The sign is the teaching. The derivative is negative everywhere, which says that increasing x always decreases 1/x. She can check that without any calculus: 1/2 > 1/3 > 1/4. The algebra and the common sense agree, and she should notice that they do.

Worked example 3 — f(x) = √x, the conjugate appears

Steps 1 and 2. The top is a difference of roots, so rationalise — move 3 from Lesson 3.

√x+h − √x h × √x+h + √x √x+h + √x

The top becomes (x+h) − x = h.

= h h(√x+h + √x) = 1 √x+h + √x

Step 4 — let h → 0.

f′(x) = 12√x

Notice what this lesson is really testing. Every first-principles sum is a Lesson 3 sum wearing a different hat. Move 1, move 3, and move 4 all appeared above. Tell her that, and the lesson stops feeling like new material.

Worked example 4 — f(x) = sin x

Step 2 uses the identity sin C − sin D = 2 cos((C+D)/2) sin((C−D)/2), from Unit 1(iii).

sin(x+h) − sin x = 2 cos(x + h2) sin h2

Step 3 — divide by h, and arrange the sine part to match Lesson 4.

= cos(x + h2) × sin(h/2) h/2

Step 4 — let h → 0. The second factor is the Lesson 4 standard form and gives 1.

f′(x) = cos x × 1 = cos x

By the same working, the derivative of cos x is −sin x. This sum is the reason Lesson 4 was taught. Say so out loud — it turns a formula she memorised into a tool she just used.

The results she may now quote

Once a derivative has been found from first principles, she may use it as a formula — unless the question says "from first principles", and then she must show all four steps.

f(x)kxxn1/x√xsin xcos x
f′(x)01n xn−1 −1/x2 1/(2√x)cos x−sin x

Note that 1/x and √x are both special cases of xn, with n = −1 and n = 1/2. She should check that herself — it turns eight facts into five.

The four mistakes this lesson prevents

1. She writes f(x+h) = f(x) + h, or (x+h)2 = x2 + h2.
The real gap: she is putting h outside the function instead of inside it. f(x+h) means: take the whole formula and replace every x by (x+h), brackets included. In her words: the recipe takes the new amount of the ingredient, not the old dish plus a pinch.

2. She puts h = 0 at step 1 or step 2.
The real gap: she is substituting before clearing the 0/0. That gives 0/0 every time. The h must survive until it has been cancelled from the bottom. This is Lesson 3 again, with the same reason: h approaches 0 and is never equal to 0.

3. She cancels the ds in dy/dx.
The real gap: she reads it as a fraction. It is one symbol.

4. She quotes n xn−1 when the question says "from first principles".
The real gap: she does not read what the question is testing. The formula gets zero marks there, however correct the answer. Teach her to underline the words "first principles" the moment she sees them.

Exercise set

  1. Easy Find the derivative of f(x) = 4x − 7 from first principles.
    Hint 1 — the type First principles on a straight line. All four steps must appear.
    Hint 2 — the move Write f(x+h) = 4(x+h) − 7, then subtract.
    Answer Top = [4x + 4h − 7] − [4x − 7] = 4h.
    Divide by h: 4. Let h → 0: f′(x) = 4.
    The answer has no x in it, and that is correct — a straight line has the same slope everywhere.
  2. Easy Find the derivative of f(x) = x2 + 3x from first principles.
    Hint 1 — the type First principles on a quadratic. Expand (x+h)2 carefully.
    Hint 2 — the move After subtracting, check that every surviving term contains an h. If one does not, find the slip before going on.
    Answer Top = [x2 + 2xh + h2 + 3x + 3h] − [x2 + 3x] = 2xh + h2 + 3h.
    Factor out h: h(2x + h + 3). Divide by h: 2x + h + 3. Let h → 0: f′(x) = 2x + 3.
  3. Medium Find the derivative of f(x) = 1/(x + 2) from first principles.
    Hint 1 — the type First principles with a fraction. The LCM move from Lesson 3 is needed on the top.
    Hint 2 — the move 1/(x+h+2) − 1/(x+2). Take the LCM as (x+h+2)(x+2).
    Hint 3 — the first line The top of the combined fraction is (x+2) − (x+h+2) = −h.
    Answer The expression becomes −h ÷ [h(x+h+2)(x+2)] = −1/[(x+h+2)(x+2)].
    Let h → 0: f′(x) = −1/(x+2)2.
    Compare it with the derivative of 1/x. The shape is identical, with x replaced by x+2 — which is exactly what she should expect.
  4. Medium Find the equation of the tangent to y = x2 at the point (2, 4).
    Hint 1 — the type Two subjects in one question: a derivative for the slope, then straight-line geometry from Unit 3(i).
    Hint 2 — the move Find dy/dx, substitute x = 2 to get the slope, then use y − y1 = m(x − x1).
    Answer dy/dx = 2x, so at x = 2 the slope is m = 4.
    y − 4 = 4(x − 2), so y = 4x − 4.
    Check it: at x = 2 the line gives y = 4, so it passes through the point. This question shape — "find the tangent at a point" — is the most likely derivative question in the ISC paper.
  5. Hard Find the derivative of f(x) = √(2x + 1) from first principles.
    Hint 1 — the type First principles with a root. Rationalise the top — move 3 from Lesson 3.
    Hint 2 — the move Multiply the top and the bottom by √(2x+2h+1) + √(2x+1).
    Hint 3 — the first line The top becomes (2x+2h+1) − (2x+1) = 2h.
    Answer After rationalising, the expression is 2h ÷ [h(√(2x+2h+1) + √(2x+1))] = 2 ÷ [√(2x+2h+1) + √(2x+1)].
    Let h → 0: 2 ÷ [2√(2x+1)] = 1/√(2x+1).
    Where the 2 came from matters. The top gave 2h rather than h because of the 2 inside the root. Next year that 2 gets a name — the chain rule — but first principles produces it with no new rule at all.
  6. Stretch Show from first principles that f(x) = |x| has no derivative at x = 0, and say what that means for the graph.
    Hint 1 — the type First principles combined with Lesson 6. The definition produces a limit, and that limit must be checked from both sides.
    Hint 2 — the move Write the definition at a = 0: [f(0+h) − f(0)]/h = |h|/h. She has met that expression before.
    Answer f(0) = 0, so the quotient is |h|/h.
    From below (h < 0) it is −1. From above (h > 0) it is +1.
    LHL ≠ RHL, so the limit does not exist, so the derivative does not exist at x = 0.
    What it means for the graph. The graph has a sharp corner at the origin. On the left the slope is −1; on the right it is +1. No single line touches the curve at that point, so there is no tangent, and therefore no slope to report.

    In her kitchen: the recipe switches at that exact amount. The cook coming from below reports one sensitivity, the cook from above reports another, and no single answer covers both.

    Why this question is here. It shows that a derivative is not guaranteed. Class 12 gives this a name — differentiability — so meeting it now, with the tool she already has, makes next year cheaper.
Close the lesson — she says this, not you

"The derivative is the limit of [f(x+h) − f(x)]/h as h goes to 0. It is the rate of change, and it is the slope of the tangent, and those are the same number. I write all four steps when the question says first principles."

Her kitchen version: "How much does the taste move for one more pinch, when the pinch is made as small as possible? A big number means a sensitive dish. Zero means one more pinch changes nothing."

If anything here is unclear

Ask. The one question worth asking early: "why can I put h = 0 at step 4 when I could not at step 1?" The answer is Lesson 3: at step 1 the h is still on the bottom, so putting h = 0 divides by zero. By step 4 the h has been cancelled from the bottom, so nothing illegal happens. If that still feels like a trick, say so — it is the single most important idea in the unit.
